Archy: A Natural History of Power Over Others
The full inquiry — mechanisms, quantities, roles, the excluded, and the
honest doubts — from the first foragers to the present
Introduction to the combined volume
This is a single inquiry into one question: how did the power of some human beings to
command, extract from, and dispose of others come into the world, harden into structure,
reinvent itself across roughly three hundred thousand years — and was it ever inevitable? The
word for that power, stripped of its prefixes, is archy (from the Greek arkhē, "rule" and also
"beginning"), the bare relation hidden inside monarchy, oligarchy, patriarchy, and hierarchy alike.
The inquiry is built in five parts, each approaching the same eight eras from a different angle,
and each correcting a distortion the others risk on their own.
Part I — A Natural History of Archy is the spine. It traces the mechanisms: the door-opening
devices — surplus and storage, the loss of exit, the defeat of leveling, control of subsistence,
inheritance, debt, the ledger, sacralization, dispersed responsibility, and the masking of coercion
as free exchange — by which power over others is generated and, occasionally, dismantled. It
establishes the framing the whole work depends on: that sustained equality, not hierarchy, was
the human baseline for most of our existence, and that archy is therefore the deviation to be
explained.
Part II — Power, Wealth, and Inequality Across the Eras makes the spine quantitative. It
asks, era by era, how big the groups were, how they were structured, what people contributed
and kept and accumulated, who pushed back, and on what grounds rulers claimed to rule —
and it carries the numbers, marked by their certainty, including the master table that runs from
door-opening mechanisms on the left to practiced archy on the right.
Part III — The Cast and the Machine supplies fixed categories for comparison. It names six
functional roles — Owners, Organizers, Enforcers, Legitimators, Producers, Dependents — that
recur in every era, tracks how they bundle at the apex and split apart, and follows the
administrative state's rise from nothing to maximal reach, asking at each step how private power
taps into the machine.
Part IV — The Excluded, the Cracks, and the Counter-Cases cuts across the eras to correct
three distortions. It disaggregates "the many" into the specific groups every hierarchy sorted to
the bottom — by gender, race, age, disability, and sexuality — and traces their subordination

through the whole institutional stack (household, religion, school, state, profession, corporation,
society). It recovers the quiet resistance — flight, sabotage, the hidden transcript — that carried
the leveling impulse through eras when revolt meant death. It puts the stateless and
non-Western societies that refused kings at the center. And it adds the material drivers — the
plow, the horse, the printing press, the engine — beneath the social story.
Part V — The Case Against This Analysis turns the strongest objections against the whole
project: that hierarchy is natural rather than a deviation, that it is efficient and funded civilization,
that inequality is the price of innovation, that the egalitarian past is romanticized, and — most
gravely, following Walter Scheidel — that inequality has only ever fallen through catastrophe,
never through peaceful reform. It concedes that the hopeful reading may be wrong, and strips
the thesis down to the more modest claim the evidence actually supports.
A note on reading order. Part I is self-contained and can be read alone. Parts II and III deepen it
and assume its framing. Part IV complicates it and is, for many readers, the heart of the work.
Part V disciplines the whole. The honest conclusion the five parts converge on is not that we are
destined for equality, nor doomed to hierarchy, but that archy is conditional — a function of
mechanisms, roles, material conditions, and choices that have been arranged many different
ways, and that remains, therefore, contestable.
A note on certainty. Throughout, claims are marked where they rest on real measurement
[measured], scholarly reconstruction [estimated], analogy or indirect evidence [inferred], or
active dispute [contested]. Certainty degrades as we go back in time; the deep past is
reconstruction, the present is measurement, and the text tries never to disguise which is which.

Part I — A Natural History of Archy
The mechanisms. This part is the spine of the whole work: how the power of one human over
another is generated and dismantled, era by era, and whether it was ever inevitable.
Preface: What "archy" is, and why the baseline matters
The word archy — from the Greek arkhē, meaning both "beginning" and "rule" — names the
thing all the familiar compounds share: monarchy, oligarchy, patriarchy, hierarchy. Strip the
prefixes and what remains is the bare relation: the institutionalized power of some humans to
command, extract from, or dispose of others. This document traces how that relation came into
the world, hardened into structure, and reinvented itself across roughly 300,000 years of human
social life.
The single most important analytical decision in a project like this is where you put the zero
point. If you assume that hierarchy is the natural human condition — that we are status-seeking
primates who build dominance ladders the moment we're left alone — then the interesting
question becomes why equality ever appears, and equality looks like the fragile, artificial
achievement. If instead you take sustained political equality as something our species not only
can do but did, for the overwhelming majority of its existence, then archy becomes the
deviation that needs explaining, and the whole inquiry inverts.
The evidence supports the second framing, with an important qualification. Foragers were not
equal because they lacked the capacity for domination — they were equal because they worked
at it, deploying active mechanisms to stop would-be dominators. This matters enormously for
everything that follows. It means archy is not the absence of a restraint that civilization later
supplied; it is the failure or dismantling of restraints that egalitarian societies had already
invented. Every later chapter is, in part, the story of how those restraints were overwhelmed,
bought off, sacralized away, or simply outscaled.
A second framing decision concerns the word "inevitable." A tempting story runs: agriculture →
surplus → storage → some people control the storage → inequality → the state, each arrow
automatic. Recent archaeology and anthropology have made this chain look far less like a law
of nature and far more like a series of contingent, reversible, often-resisted transitions. The
honest position is neither "inequality was always coming" nor "inequality was a single
catastrophic wrong turn," but something harder: humans have repeatedly built hierarchy and
repeatedly dismantled it, and the ratchet only locked in particular places under particular
conditions. Where this document can show the ratchet not locking — societies that had cities

-- 3 of 74 --

without kings, surplus without aristocracy, scale without servitude — those cases are doing the
heaviest analytical lifting, because they break the spell of inevitability.
A note on method. This synthesizes archaeology, anthropology, history, and economics, and
these fields disagree. Where there's a live scholarly fight — and there are several — the text
flags it rather than papering over it. The aim is not a single tidy thesis but a map of the
mechanisms: the recurring devices by which power over others is generated, stored,
transmitted, legitimated, and resisted.
Era 1 — Forager egalitarianism
Deep prehistory – c. 12,000 BCE: the baseline, and how it was actively maintained
For something like 95% of the time anatomically modern humans have existed, people lived in
small, mobile groups without rulers, without inherited rank, without the institutional means for
one person to compel another. To call this "equality" can mislead a modern ear — it does not
mean everyone was the same, or that there were no differences in skill, influence, or esteem. It
means something more specific and more interesting: there was no stable, transmissible,
coercive power of one person over another. Influence existed; command did not.
What the equality actually consisted of
Three things were genuinely flat in mobile forager life, and it's worth separating them because
later eras pry them apart one by one.
The first is material. With frequent movement, accumulation is a burden, not an asset — you
carry what you own. There is little durable wealth to hoard and nothing like land tenure when the
band ranges over a territory it does not individually parcel. The second is political: decisions
were reached by discussion, persuasion, and consensus, and individuals retained the option to
simply leave — to walk to another band — which is the most powerful check on a bully that
exists. The third is informational and economic: in a sharing economy organized around the
daily distribution of hunted and gathered food, no one controls the others' subsistence. You
cannot starve someone into obedience when everyone has independent access to the food
supply and a strong norm compels its sharing.
The key insight: equality was performed, not passive
Here is the finding that overturns the "natural ladder" intuition. Forager equality was not the
placid default of creatures too simple to dominate. It was a vigilant political achievement,
maintained by what the anthropologist Christopher Boehm called reverse dominance
hierarchy: the rank and file, the would-be subordinates, collectively dominate anyone who tries
to rise above them. The many keep the one down. This is the mirror image of the ape troop, and
it is everywhere in the ethnographic record of mobile foragers.

The toolkit of leveling mechanisms is remarkably consistent across unrelated societies on
different continents, which suggests it answers a recurring problem rather than reflecting a
single culture:
- Mockery and ridicule. The arrogant or domineering are teased relentlessly. Humor is a
precision instrument for deflating status without open conflict.
- Insulting the meat. Among the Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) of the Kalahari, a hunter who brings
in a large kill is mocked for bringing something so scrawny and worthless. The hunter is
expected to disparage his own success. The point, as one Ju/'hoan man famously
explained to the anthropologist Richard Lee, is explicit: when a young man kills much
meat he comes to think of himself as a chief or a big man, and he thinks of the rest as
his inferiors — so "we refuse one who boasts," and we cool his heart and make him
gentle by calling his meat worthless. The leveling is theorized by its practitioners. They
know exactly what they are preventing.
- Demand sharing. The norm that anyone may ask for a portion, and refusal is a grave
offense, means surplus cannot pool in one set of hands.
- Disengagement and mobility. Fission — splitting off — is always available, so no one
is trapped under a domineering personality.
- The ultimate sanctions. Where ridicule and ostracism fail against a genuinely
dangerous, violent, or domineering individual, the ethnographic record documents the
group resorting to expulsion and, in extremis, sanctioned execution of the upstart, often
carried out by a close kinsman to forestall a feud.
Inequality of esteem persisted — good hunters, wise elders, gifted storytellers, skilled mediators
were admired and listened to. But esteem was not convertible into command, and crucially it
was not heritable. A great hunter's son inherited no office. The conversion of fleeting prestige
into durable, transmissible power is precisely the thing these societies blocked, and precisely
the thing later societies would learn to enable.
Complication: foragers were not all the same
The picture above describes mobile, immediate-return foragers — those who consume what
they get rather than storing it. But the foraging world was far more varied, and the variation is
the seed of everything later. Delayed-return foragers — who invest in fixed facilities like fish
weirs, smokehouses, and stored caches — show, in some cases, marked hierarchy. The most
famous are the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast (Kwakwaka'wakw, Tlingit,
Haida and others): sedentary or semi-sedentary, living off astonishingly rich salmon runs, with
hereditary nobility, commoners, and chattel slavery — without any agriculture at all. Their wealth
was displayed and redistributed through the potlatch, a competitive feast at which a chief gave
away or destroyed enormous quantities of goods to assert rank.
This is a hinge fact. It shows that the trigger for hierarchy is not farming as such but a cluster
— storable concentrated resources, reduced mobility, defensible wealth — that farming would

later supply reliably but that nature occasionally supplied on its own. It also shows that mobility
and the right to leave are doing much of the egalitarian work: take them away, and rank can
grow even among hunter-gatherers.
The deep-history complication: maybe it wasn't monotonous at all
The traditional account treats the Palaeolithic as one long uniform plateau of small egalitarian
bands. A newer body of work, associated with the archaeologist David Wengrow and the late
anthropologist David Graeber, argues that the deep past was politically experimental and
seasonal. The richly furnished Upper Palaeolithic burials of Sungir (in Russia, c. 34,000 years
ago) — children laid out with tens of thousands of painstakingly drilled mammoth-ivory beads —
and the monumental hunter-gatherer sanctuary at Göbekli Tepe in Anatolia (c. 9500 BCE,
pre-agricultural) suggest concentrations of labor, ritual authority, or status that don't fit a flat
band model. Their reading: some Ice Age societies may have oscillated, assembling into large
hierarchical formations for part of the year (a ceremonial season, a herd hunt) and dispersing
into small egalitarian groups for the rest — consciously moving between political modes. On this
view the human political repertoire was capacious and self-aware from very early, and
permanent hierarchy is less a discovery than a getting stuck in one season of a cycle humans
had long known how to exit.
This is contested — the lavish burials might mark unusual individuals rather than institutions,
and seasonal readings strain some of the evidence — but even the cautious version dislodges
the comfortable assumption that our ancestors simply didn't know how to be unequal until
farming taught them. They knew. They mostly chose otherwise, and built tools to enforce the
choice.
What this baseline establishes
Three conclusions carry forward and structure everything below.
First, the human capacity for both domination and its suppression is ancient and
co-present. We are not by nature levelers or by nature climbers; we are both, and which one
prevails is a matter of institutions and conditions, not biology.
First among the conditions: archy requires that the leveling mechanisms be defeated.
Mockery, sharing, mobility, the right to leave — each of these will be neutralized, one at a time,
in the chapters ahead. Watch for the moment in each era when leaving stops being an option,
because that is usually the moment archy locks.
And third, the trigger is not any single technology but a configuration — concentrated,
storable, defensible resources plus reduced exit. The Neolithic is about to supply that
configuration on a vast scale and make it permanent. Whether it had to produce kings is the
question of the next part.

Era 2 — The Neolithic threshold
c. 12,000 – 4,000 BCE: settlement, storage, surplus, and the contested birth of
inequality
This is the pivot of the whole story, and the place where the word "inevitable" must either earn
its keep or be retired. The old account is clean and powerful, which is why it became the default.
It runs like this: people began farming; farming produced more food than was immediately
needed; surplus had to be stored; stored surplus was concentrated and seizable in a way that a
daily catch of game never was; whoever controlled the stores controlled the people who needed
them; control hardened into office, office into rank, rank into the state. Sedentism also ended the
great equalizer of mobility — you cannot vote with your feet when your fields, your granary, and
your ancestors' graves are here. The leveling mechanisms of Era 1 quietly died: you cannot
fission away from a field you have cleared over a decade, and demand-sharing cannot survive a
harvest that one household grew and another did not.
Every link in that chain is real. The problem is that the chain did not, in fact, snap shut
everywhere it could have, and the exceptions are now too numerous and too well-documented
to treat as noise.
What actually happened to time and resistance
The Neolithic was not an event but a process drawn out over millennia, and it did not begin with
farming. The earliest monumental site we know, Göbekli Tepe, was built by hunter-gatherers
around 9500 BCE — massive carved stone pillars arranged by people who had not yet
domesticated their staple crops. Whatever authority coordinated that labor, it preceded the
granary. Conversely, many early farming communities show a stubborn commitment to equality
long after they had the surplus that supposedly makes hierarchy automatic.
The most spectacular case is Çatalhöyük in central Anatolia (c. 7100–5700 BCE), a town of
several thousand people packed into nearly identical mudbrick houses entered through the roof.
For something like a thousand years — far longer than most later dynasties last — this large,
dense, fully agricultural community shows almost no architectural evidence of rank: no palace,
no elite quarter, no rich-versus-poor housing, no monumental tombs. Houses are strikingly
uniform in size and contents. People were doing the supposedly impossible: living packed
together, farming, storing food, and refusing to let hierarchy crystallize, apparently through a
powerful ideology of household equivalence and ancestral continuity (the dead were buried
beneath the floors of the homes). Çatalhöyük is the single most important piece of evidence
against strong inevitability, because it removes every excuse — they had the surplus, the
density, and the sedentism, and still the ratchet did not lock for a millennium.
The slow, reversible, resisted character of the transition

The transition was slow and partial. People practiced "play farming" — cultivating some plants
casually for centuries or millennia without committing to full agricultural dependence, keeping a
foot in the foraging world. The notion of a sudden "Agricultural Revolution" that flipped a switch
is an artifact of looking at the deep past through a telescope that compresses three thousand
years into a sentence.
The transition was reversible. Societies took up farming and then abandoned it; took up
hierarchy and then dismantled it. Some Neolithic communities show a phase of emergent
inequality followed by a deliberate return to flatter arrangements — the ratchet running
backwards. This is fatal to any model in which inequality, once it appears, can only grow.
And the transition was resisted. The leveling instinct of Era 1 did not vanish when people
settled down; it adapted, and you can read the struggle in the ground. Across Neolithic Europe
the great communal megalithic tombs — long barrows, passage graves, the dolmens — pool
many bodies together anonymously, an architecture that asserts the collective over any
individual, the community of ancestors over any single lineage's claim to greatness. This is the
leveling ethos rendered in stone, a deliberate refusal of the individuating monument. The
contrast with what comes next — the single richly furnished royal grave shouting one person's
supremacy — measures exactly how far archy had to travel and how hard it was pushed back.
The mechanisms that did generate inequality, where it took
When inequality did take root, it ran through identifiable channels, and naming them precisely
matters because the same channels recur for the next ten thousand years.
Storage and the calendar of scarcity. A granary is a battery of stored power. Whoever
controls access to it controls the hungry gap between harvests — the lean season when last
year's grain is the only thing between a family and starvation. This is the first time in human
history that one person can systematically command another by controlling the food supply, the
exact lever that the forager sharing economy had been engineered to deny anyone.
The house, the heir, and inheritance. Sedentism creates the durable household as an
economic unit, and the durable household creates the heritable estate — fields cleared, animals
bred, granaries built, all of it now passing from parents to children. Inheritance is the device that
converts a one-generation advantage into a dynasty. The forager prohibition on heritable rank is
overwhelmed not by force but by the simple accumulation of transmissible things.
Prestige goods and the debt of the feast. Long-distance trade in rare valuables — obsidian,
fine stone, later copper and gold — let aspiring leaders accumulate wealth that was portable,
durable, and impressive. The competitive feast (the Neolithic ancestor of the potlatch) let them
convert that wealth into followers: a lavish feast puts the guests in a debt of obligation they can
only discharge through loyalty and labor. This is the "aggrandizer" or "big man" pathway
theorized by the archaeologist Brian Hayden — ambitious individuals exploiting surplus to build

webs of obligation, turning generosity itself into a tool of domination. The forager who insulted
the meat to deflate the boaster was defending against exactly this, and in the Neolithic, with
stored surplus to draw on, the boaster finally had the resources to win.
Animals, herds, and movable wealth. Domesticated livestock are wealth that breeds, that can
be counted, raided, given as bridewealth, and accumulated without limit. In much of the world
the herd, not the field, was the first great engine of inequality — and pastoralism produced its
own steep hierarchies and its own warrior aristocracies.
The verdict on inevitability
The honest synthesis is this. The Neolithic supplied the necessary conditions for durable archy
— storable surplus, reduced mobility, heritable estates, the death of demand-sharing — and
once those conditions were widespread, hierarchy became available in a way it had never been
before. But "available" is not "inevitable." Çatalhöyük had every necessary condition and stayed
flat for a thousand years. Other communities built inequality and then chose to tear it down. The
conditions loaded the dice; they did not throw them.
What the Neolithic really did was raise the stakes and weaken the defenses. The leveling
mechanisms still operated, but they were fighting on worse terrain: against heritable wealth,
against the granary's quiet coercion, against the feast's web of debt, and without the trump card
of easy exit. Sometimes they won, for centuries. But the terrain now favored the aggrandizer,
and over the long run, in the richest and most crowded river valleys, the defenses would be not
merely defeated but inverted — turned into their opposite, so that the very ideology of
community and sharing would be captured and redeployed to justify a king who fed his people
from a granary he owned. That inversion is the work of the next part.
Era 3 — The first states and the god-kings
c. 4,000 – 800 BCE: writing, temples, taxation, and the sacralization of command
Somewhere in the fourth millennium BCE, in southern Mesopotamia and then the Nile valley,
the available hierarchy of the late Neolithic became something qualitatively new: the state. Not
merely a rich man with followers, but a standing apparatus — offices that outlived their holders,
a class that did not farm, a machinery for extracting and recording the labor of everyone else.
This is the threshold at which archy stops being a recurring social tendency and becomes an
institution with the power to reproduce itself across generations regardless of the personalities
involved. Understanding how it locked in here — and why it did not lock in everywhere
comparable — is the core of this part.

Four technologies of permanent power
The early states ran on a small set of interlocking inventions, each of which solved a problem
that had previously kept hierarchy fragile.
Writing, born as accountancy. It is one of the most clarifying facts in all of history that writing
was not invented to record poetry, law, or prayer. It was invented to keep accounts. The earliest
texts from Uruk, around 3300 BCE, are administrative: lists of grain, livestock, laborers, and
rations, impressed into clay by temple bureaucrats. Before writing, memory limited how much
could be owed, owned, and tracked; a debt could be forgotten, a tribute disputed. Writing made
obligation permanent and impersonal — it let the state remember what no human could, and
remember it against you. The ledger is the foundational instrument of the state, and it appears
at the very beginning. Almost every later technology of control, down to the database, is a
descendant of the Uruk grain tablet.
The temple and the granary as one institution. In early Mesopotamia the temple was not only
a house of the god; it was the largest landowner, the central granary, the employer, the
redistributor. The god owned the city; the priests managed the god's estate; the people worked
the god's land and received rations. This fusion is decisive. It means that from the very start,
economic extraction and cosmic order were the same system. To withhold your labor or your
grain was not tax evasion — it was sacrilege, a tear in the fabric of the cosmos. The granary
that the Neolithic aggrandizer controlled by force of personality is now controlled by the god
himself, and disobedience is not defiance of a man but of the order of being.
Taxation, tribute, and the corvée. The state's defining capacity is to extract — a portion of the
harvest, a share of the herd, and above all labor: the corvée, the obligation to work on the ruler's
projects. The pyramids, the ziggurats, the canals and walls were built on conscripted labor, and
that labor is the surplus of Era 2 made compulsory and monumental. The forager who could
walk away now owes a season of digging to a king he will never see.
The ruler–priest pairing and the divine charter. The single greatest problem for any system
of archy is legitimacy: why should the many obey the few, when the many vastly outnumber
them and once collectively dominated any upstart? Naked force cannot sustain rule — there are
never enough soldiers, and soldiers themselves must be given a reason. The early states
solved this with the most powerful idea in the political history of the species: the ruler is divine,
or divinely chosen. The Egyptian pharaoh was a god, the living Horus, the guarantor of ma'at —
cosmic order, justice, the rising of the sun and the flooding of the Nile. The Mesopotamian king
ruled as the god's chosen steward. To resist was not rebellion but chaos, the unmaking of the
world.
This is the deep inversion promised at the end of Era 2. The forager leveling ideology held that
no one should rise above the group; the god-king ideology holds that one man embodies the
group, the cosmos, and the divine, so that obedience to him is membership in the order of

things. The same human impulse toward unity and belonging that once kept everyone equal is
captured and turned into the keystone of inequality. The leveling mechanism is not merely
defeated — it is reversed and conscripted.
Debt: the recorded chain, and the device that broke it
Here the story acquires its most modern-feeling thread. The same writing that recorded grain
recorded debt — and debt is arguably the most important single mechanism in this entire
history, because it is the device that converts a temporary misfortune into permanent bondage
with the victim's own consent.
The mechanism is brutally simple and recurs for the next four thousand years. A farmer has a
bad year; he borrows grain or silver to survive or to pay his tax; the loan carries interest
(Mesopotamian rates were savage); he cannot repay; the creditor takes his collateral — first his
oxen, then his land, then his children sold into debt-servitude, then the farmer himself. The
anthropologist David Graeber argued that debt, not barter, is the true origin of money and
markets, and that debt-bondage is the original and most persistent engine of the unfree labor
that runs through all later history. Crucially, debt moralizes extraction: the debtor agreed, the
debtor owes, the debtor's suffering is the debtor's own fault. It is a far cheaper tool of domination
than force, because it recruits the victim's own sense of obligation.
What makes the early Near East so revealing is that the rulers understood this dynamic and
intervened against it — not from compassion but from cold political sense. When too many
farmers fell into debt-bondage, the productive population that paid taxes and served in the army
was hollowed out, and the wealth and people drained into the hands of private creditors who
rivaled the throne. So the kings periodically declared debt cancellations: the Mesopotamian
clean slate or (an)durarum, in which non-commercial debts were annulled, debt-slaves freed,
and forfeited land returned to its original holders. A new king often proclaimed one on taking the
throne. The Hebrew Jubilee of Leviticus — every fiftieth year, debts forgiven, bondsmen freed,
ancestral land restored — is the same institution rendered as sacred law, the word deror a direct
cognate of the Akkadian durarum.
The jubilee is one of the most important facts in this document, for three reasons. It proves the
ancients knew their economy generated bondage as a matter of structure, not accident. It
shows that the response was not to abolish the debt mechanism but to periodically reset it — to
relieve the pressure without dismantling the machine. And it establishes the deep pattern of all
later history: archy generates a counter-current of relief and resistance, and that counter-current
is itself often managed from above, a safety valve that preserves the system by venting its worst
pressures. The leveling impulse of Era 1 survives here, but no longer as the band ridiculing a
boaster — now as a king periodically freeing the debtors, having first presided over the system
that bound them.

The crucial counter-cases: states without kings
If Mesopotamia and Egypt were the whole story, inevitability would win by default. They are not.
The same period offers societies that built cities, surplus, and complexity without the god-king
package, and these break the chain.
The Indus Valley civilization (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro; c. 2600–1900 BCE) was vast — bigger
in area than Egypt or Mesopotamia — with sophisticated planned cities, standardized weights,
advanced sanitation, and long-distance trade. Yet it has yielded no convincing palaces, no royal
tombs, no monumental depictions of rulers, no grand individual burials, no clear iconography of
a king at all. Its undeciphered script and conspicuous absence of glorified individuals have led a
number of archaeologists to argue it was organized on a strikingly different and more corporate
or distributed basis. Whatever it was, it was not a god-king state, and it lasted for centuries.
Early Chinese civilization followed its own path and its own timing. The Shang (c. 1600–1046
BCE) were a powerful sacrificial-warrior monarchy whose kings communicated with royal
ancestors through oracle-bone divination — a different sacral logic from Egypt's, with authority
running through the ancestral line rather than through the king's own divinity, and underwritten
by large-scale human sacrifice. The succeeding Zhou then produced one of the most
consequential legitimating ideas in world history, the Mandate of Heaven: Heaven grants the
right to rule to the just, and withdraws it from the cruel, so that a dynasty's overthrow proves it
had already lost the Mandate. This was a sacral charter for kingship like the others — but with a
revolutionary clause built in, because it made rule conditional on conduct and supplied, for all
later Chinese history, a ready-made justification for rebellion against a tyrant. Sacralization here
cut both ways.
And in the Americas, complexity was emerging on a completely independent track — the Norte
Chico/Caral sites on the Peruvian coast (c. 3000 BCE and after) raising monumental platform
mounds, and the Olmec of Mesoamerica (from c. 1500 BCE) carving the colossal stone heads
that probably depict rulers. Independent invention of monumental hierarchy on a separate
continent tells us the pathway is a recurrent human possibility — but the Indus contrast on the
same timeline tells us it was never the only one.
What this era establishes
The first states convert the available hierarchy of the Neolithic into a self-reproducing institution,
and they do it with four tools — the ledger, the temple-granary, compulsory extraction, and the
divine charter — that together solve hierarchy's two chronic weaknesses: memory (the ledger
remembers obligation forever) and legitimacy (the god makes obedience sacred). Debt emerges
as the master-mechanism of bondage, and the jubilee as the master-mechanism of managed
relief. And the counter-cases — Indus, the conditional Mandate, the varied Americas —
establish that even at this threshold there was no single road. But where the god-king package
did lock in, it would prove almost unimaginably durable, the template for kingship for the next
three thousand years.

Era 4 — Classical empires and slavery
c. 800 BCE – 500 CE: codified law, coinage, mass slavery, and citizenship as graded
belonging
The classical world did not invent archy, but it systematized it — gave it written law, abstract
money, philosophical justification, and an industrial scale of human bondage. Where the
god-king ruled by being the cosmos, the classical empires increasingly ruled through institutions
that outlasted any ruler: a body of law, a coinage, a citizenship, a slave system. Power became
less a person and more a structure, which is why so much of the classical apparatus feels eerily
continuous with our own.
Law: the double edge of writing it down
The early states had decrees; the classical world produced codified law — comprehensive,
public, written rules. The trajectory runs from Hammurabi's stele (c. 1754 BCE, strictly
pre-classical but the ancestor) through Solon's reforms at Athens, the Roman Twelve Tables (c.
451 BCE), and ultimately the vast edifice of Roman jurisprudence that would be compiled in
Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis and shape European law for two millennia.
Codified law cuts both ways, and that ambivalence is the point. On one edge, writing the law
down limits arbitrary power: the rule is public, predictable, the same on Tuesday as on Monday,
and even the powerful can be held to it. The Roman plebeians demanded the Twelve Tables
precisely so that patrician magistrates could no longer simply invent the law against them. Law
is, in this aspect, a descendant of the leveling impulse — a check on the strong. On the other
edge, codified law entrenches hierarchy by giving it the majesty and permanence of writing:
Roman law meticulously defined the gradations of person and the absolute property of the
master in the slave, encoding subordination as eternal legal fact rather than mere custom. The
same instrument that restrains the powerful also legitimates and stabilizes their power. This
double edge will reappear in every later legal order, including our own constitutions.
Coinage: abstract, impersonal, universal power
Around the seventh century BCE, in Lydia and the Greek world, and independently in China and
India, came coined money — standardized stamped metal, guaranteed by the state, usable by
anyone. Its consequences for archy are profound and underappreciated.
Coinage makes wealth abstract, portable, anonymous, and infinitely accumulable. A granary of
barley rots and is tied to a place; a hoard of silver coin is liquid power that can be moved,
hidden, lent at interest, and piled up without limit. Coinage also vastly extends the reach of debt
— the master-mechanism of Era 3 — by making it precisely calculable and universally
enforceable. And coinage and empire grew together in a self-feeding loop that Graeber called

-- 13 of 74 --

the military-coinage-slavery complex: the state mints coin to pay soldiers; it requires taxes be
paid back in that coin, forcing everyone into the market to obtain it; soldiers spend coin on
plunder including slaves; slaves work the mines that produce the metal for more coin. Money,
war, and bondage became a single integrated machine for converting human beings and
territory into liquid, accumulable power.
Mass slavery: archy at industrial scale
Slavery existed before — debt-bondage in Mesopotamia, captives on the Northwest Coast —
but classical Greece and Rome made it the structural foundation of the economy and of citizen
life itself. These were genuine slave societies, not merely societies that had slaves: a large
fraction of the population was chattel — legally a thing, a "speaking tool" in the Roman phrase,
owned absolutely, bought and sold, worked in mines and on the great estates (latifundia) and in
households. Rome's wars of conquest were also vast slave-harvesting operations, funneling
captives by the hundreds of thousands into Italy.
The intellectual move that accompanied this is one of the darkest in the history of ideas.
Aristotle, in the Politics, argued that some people are slaves by nature — beings who lack the
full rational faculty and are therefore better off governed by a master, for whom slavery is fitting
and even beneficial. This is the first great theory of natural hierarchy: the claim that
subordination reflects a real, inborn difference of kind, that the order of domination maps onto
the order of nature. It is the ancestor of every later doctrine — racial, gendered, civilizational —
that justifies the power of one group over another by appeal to their essential nature. The
god-king said obey because I am divine; Aristotle says obey because you are, by nature, the
kind of thing that is ruled. The second claim is more insidious because it locates the justification
inside the dominated themselves.
The counter-current was present but weak. Spartacus's revolt (73–71 BCE) terrified Rome but
sought escape, not abolition; no classical movement proposed ending slavery as such. The
Stoics developed a striking idea of inner freedom and the brotherhood of all rational beings —
Epictetus, who had himself been a slave, taught that the master can chain the body but never
the will — yet Stoicism preached endurance and inner liberty rather than the overthrow of the
institution. The horizon of abolition did not yet exist. This absence is itself important: it shows
that even a brilliant civilization can find a monstrous institution simply unquestionable,
naturalized so completely that the alternative is unthinkable. The leveling impulse had been not
just defeated but philosophized out of view.
Citizenship: belonging as a hierarchy
The classical world's most enduring contribution to the architecture of archy may be the idea
that belonging itself is ranked. Athenian democracy — radical and real for those inside it —
drew a hard line: adult male citizens governed; women, resident foreigners (metics), and the
enslaved majority did not. The democracy of the few rested on the labor and exclusion of the
many, and the equality inside the citizen body was inseparable from the inequality at its edge.

-- 14 of 74 --

Rome turned this into a sophisticated, graded system. Citizenship was not binary but a ladder of
statuses — full citizen, Latin right, freedman, provincial subject, slave — each with different
bundles of rights, and movement up the ladder was possible (a freed slave's son could be a
citizen; the Edict of Caracalla in 212 CE extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the
empire). This was genuinely more flexible than caste, but it established a template of immense
and lasting power: the sorting of human beings into legal tiers of belonging, with rights attached
to tier rather than to humanity. The modern world's passports, immigration categories, and
citizenship regimes are the direct institutional descendants. Citizenship is the velvet form of
archy — it can feel like inclusion to those inside while functioning as exclusion at the boundary.
The Eastern parallel: Han China and the bureaucratic alternative
Contemporary with Rome, Han China (206 BCE – 220 CE) ran an empire of comparable scale
on a strikingly different principle, and the contrast illuminates what is contingent in the Western
path. Where Rome rested on slave latifundia and a citizen-soldier ideology, the Han governed
through a centralized bureaucracy staffed increasingly by men selected for their mastery of texts
— the embryo of the examination system that would later become the famous meritocratic civil
service. Confucian ideology legitimated hierarchy not through a slave/free binary or a divine king
but through a web of reciprocal but unequal duties: ruler and minister, father and son, husband
and wife, each relationship binding both parties but never as equals. Subordination was framed
as harmony, role, and mutual obligation rather than ownership.
This matters for the inevitability question because it shows the classical solution to large-scale
order was plural. Hierarchy was universal across the Eurasian empires, but its technology and
justification varied profoundly — chattel slavery and codified property in the West, bureaucratic
office and reciprocal Confucian duty in the East, the Persian Achaemenid model of a "king of
kings" presiding tolerantly over a mosaic of subject peoples in between. Same outcome — the
power of the few over the many at imperial scale — reached by genuinely different roads, which
means no single road was the road.
What this era establishes
The classical empires depersonalize archy. Power moves from the body of the god-king into
durable institutions — law, coin, citizenship, the slave system — that run regardless of who sits
on the throne, and that prove able to operate at a scale of millions. Three inventions here are
permanent additions to the repertoire: abstract money (limitless, liquid, accumulable power),
natural-hierarchy theory (subordination as inborn fact), and graded citizenship (belonging as
rank). Slavery reaches an industrial scale and acquires a philosophy. And the counter-current
narrows to almost nothing — endurance, inner freedom, the occasional desperate revolt — with
the very idea of abolition still below the horizon. That horizon will not open for another thousand
years and more, and when it does it will be against a slavery even larger and crueler than
Rome's.

Era 5 — Feudal and tributary orders
c. 500 – 1450: land-bound labor, divine right, and the many faces of the unfree
After the western Roman state dissolved, archy in much of Eurasia took a recognizable new
shape: power devolved, fragmented, and localized, attaching itself directly to land and to the
bodies that worked it. The grand impersonal machinery of the classical empire — the standing
bureaucracy, the universal coinage, the codified law reaching every province — receded in the
European west, and into the vacuum came a more intimate, more personal, and in some ways
more total form of domination. The defining fact of this whole era, across very different
civilizations, is the binding of labor to land: the worker is no longer chattel that can be sold
away, but neither is he free to leave. The forager's ultimate weapon — exit, the right to walk
away — is abolished not by selling the person but by fixing him to the soil.
This part deliberately ranges beyond Europe, because the European manor is only one species
of a genus that appeared, with local logic, across the medieval world.
European feudalism and serfdom
The classic model — never as tidy in reality as in the textbook — was a pyramid of personal
bonds. The king granted land (a fief) to great lords in exchange for military service and loyalty;
lords sub-granted to lesser knights; at the base, the serf worked the lord's manor. The serf was
not a slave: he could not be sold as an individual, he held customary rights to work strips of land
and to the protection of his lord, he had a family and a household the law recognized. But he
was bound to the manor — he could not leave without permission, owed days of labor on the
lord's demesne (the descendant of the corvée), paid dues in kind and later in cash, and was
subject to the lord's court. He was, in the precise medieval phrase, adscriptus glebae — written
into the soil, transferred with the land when it changed hands.
The legitimating ideology was the doctrine of the three orders: those who pray (oratores —
clergy), those who fight (bellatores — nobility), and those who work (laboratores — everyone
else). This was presented as a divinely ordained division of function in which each order served
the others — the peasant fed the knight who protected him and the priest who prayed for his
soul. It is the Confucian logic of reciprocal-but-unequal duty in Christian dress, and like all the
great legitimations it converted raw extraction into cosmic harmony and mutual obligation.
Above the whole structure sat the divine right of kings: the monarch answerable to God alone,
the direct descendant of the god-king charter of Era 3, persisting essentially unbroken from
Sumer to Versailles.
The Church was not outside this system but central to it — itself a vast landlord with its own
serfs, the ideological author of the three orders, and the keeper of the keys to salvation, which
gave it a coercive power over the medieval mind at least as great as the lord's power over the
body. Heaven and hell are, among other things, instruments of social control: the promise that

-- 16 of 74 --

earthly subordination would be rewarded, and earthly rebellion punished, in an eternity that no
one could verify but everyone feared.
The same problem solved elsewhere: the genus of tributary power
The European arrangement was one local answer to a problem every complex agrarian society
faced — how a small armed-and-literate elite extracts a surplus from a large peasant majority.
Naming the parallel answers is essential, both to avoid Eurocentrism and to see what is
structural rather than peculiar.
Japan developed, largely independently, a strikingly convergent system. By the time of the
shogunate, real power lay with a military aristocracy — the daimyō lords and their samurai
retainers — bound by codes of loyalty and service strongly analogous to European vassalage,
presiding over peasants tied to the land and the village, with an emperor reduced to a sacral
figurehead. That two civilizations with almost no contact produced such similar structures is
strong evidence that this configuration is a recurrent solution to the agrarian-extraction problem,
not a cultural accident.
The Islamic world ran a different and often more centralized variant. Rather than hereditary
fiefs, many Islamic states used the iqṭāʿ — a grant of the right to collect the tax revenue of a
district, assigned to a soldier or official in lieu of salary, in principle revocable and
non-hereditary, which kept power flowing through the state rather than congealing into an
independent landed nobility. Strikingly, some of these states ran on military slavery — the
mamlūk and later Ottoman devshirme systems, in which boys were taken, converted, and
trained into elite slave-soldiers and administrators who could rise to the very pinnacle of power,
even to rule. A "slave" who commands armies and governs provinces explodes any simple
notion of what bondage means: status here was a matter of belonging to the ruler's household
rather than of degradation, a logic almost unintelligible in Roman or later Atlantic terms, and a
reminder that "slavery" names very different institutions in different systems.
India's structure was the most distinctive and, in one respect, the most extreme: the caste
system (varna and the thousands of local jāti). Here social hierarchy was not merely heritable
but ritually sacralized as a cosmic order of purity and pollution, fixed at birth, regulating whom
one could marry, eat with, and what work one must do, and underwritten by religious doctrines
of dharma (duty appropriate to one's station) and rebirth (one's birth-station reflecting deeds in
past lives). Caste is the most thoroughgoing naturalization of hierarchy in human history —
more total than Aristotle's natural slavery, because it embeds rank in the structure of the cosmos
and the soul's own karmic history, making subordination not just natural but deserved across
lifetimes. The leveling impulse faces, here, its most formidable ideological adversary: a system
in which to resist your station is to defy the moral order of the universe itself.
The peasant economy and the counter-currents
Across all these systems the underlying reality was a tributary mode: peasants produced, and
a portion was extracted upward as rent, tax, tribute, labor, or some blend, to support a

-- 17 of 74 --

non-producing elite of warriors, priests, and officials. The mechanisms of Era 3 — extraction,
sacralization, the binding of labor — persisted; what changed was their localization (power
attached to land and lord rather than to a distant capital) and the form of unfreedom (bound
rather than owned).
But the leveling impulse was never extinguished, and the medieval world saw it flare repeatedly.
Peasant revolts punctuate the era — the English Rising of 1381, the German Peasants' War of
1525, the jacqueries of France, the Red Turban and countless other risings in China — often
explicitly egalitarian, demanding the abolition of serfdom and sometimes invoking a radical
theology: the famous English question "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the
gentleman?" — if we all descend from one couple who worked the earth with their own hands,
where did lords come from? This is the leveling ethos of Era 1, surfacing across ten thousand
years, now armed with scripture: the claim that hierarchy is a human imposition, not a divine or
natural fact, that there was an original equality and the lords are usurpers of it. Most revolts
were crushed, but they register the permanent presence of the egalitarian counter-current even
under the most sacralized hierarchies.
And then, in the European case, came a demographic accident with vast structural
consequences. The Black Death (1347–51) killed perhaps a third to half of Europe's people,
and by making labor suddenly scarce it gave the surviving peasants enormous bargaining
power. Lords who needed workers had to compete for them; wages rose, dues fell, and across
much of Western Europe serfdom withered over the following centuries — not primarily through
revolt but through the silent leverage of a labor shortage. The forager's lost weapon, exit,
partially returned: when labor is scarce and mobile, the bound worker can again, in effect,
threaten to leave, and the binding loosens. (In Eastern Europe, by contrast, lords responded to
the same pressures by tightening serfdom — the "second serfdom" — a divergence that shaped
the continent for centuries and warns against assuming any single direction to history.) This is a
profound structural lesson that will recur in Era 7: the relative scarcity or abundance of labor
is one of the deepest determinants of how much the powerful can extract, often more
decisive than ideology or law.
What this era establishes
The feudal-tributary millennium shows archy localizing and personalizing after the fall of the
classical megastate, binding labor to land across civilizations through convergent but distinct
institutions — European serfdom, Japanese vassalage, the Islamic iqṭāʿ and military slavery,
Indian caste. It demonstrates, through that convergence, that the
small-elite-extracting-from-a-peasant-majority structure is a recurrent solution rather than a
cultural quirk. It shows ideology reaching its maximum naturalizing power in caste. And it
reveals two engines of the counter-current that will dominate the modern story: the recurring
egalitarian revolt with its memory of an original equality, and the brute leverage of labor scarcity,
which can loosen the chains without anyone intending it. As this era closes, Europe — newly
freed of serfdom in its west, hungry for the silver that pays its debts, and armed with ships — is

-- 18 of 74 --

about to take its hierarchies global, and to invent a form of bondage worse than anything yet
seen.
Era 6 — Mercantilism, colonialism, and the Atlantic system
c. 1450 – 1800: the joint-stock company, racialized slavery, and power going global
From roughly 1450 a regional story becomes a planetary one. European powers, propelled by
ship technology, gunpowder, the hunger for silver to settle their debts and buy Asian goods, and
a Christianity that licensed conquest of the "heathen," built the first genuinely global system of
extraction. Archy now operates across oceans and continents, links the silver mines of the
Andes to the spice ports of Asia to the sugar islands of the Caribbean, and invents two devices
of staggering and lasting consequence: the corporation as an engine of impersonal
accumulation, and racialized chattel slavery as a system of bondage justified by a newly
invented hierarchy of human kinds.
The joint-stock company: archy without a body, accountability without a soul
The instrument that made global extraction possible was a legal-financial innovation whose
importance is hard to overstate: the joint-stock chartered company, above all the English and
Dutch East India Companies (founded 1600 and 1602). It solved a specific problem — how to
assemble the enormous capital and bear the enormous risk of long-distance armed trade — by
pooling investment from many shareholders, who owned tradable shares and enjoyed limited
liability, and by persisting as an entity beyond the lives of its members.
The consequences for the structure of power were revolutionary, and they point straight at our
own age. The company is an immortal, impersonal accumulator — it never dies, never tires, has
no body to imprison and no soul to damn, and exists for the single purpose of returning profit to
its shareholders. It separated ownership from management (shareholders owned; directors ran
it), creating a structure where the people who controlled the machine were not personally the
people who owned it, and neither bore the full human consequences of what the machine did.
And the great companies were granted sovereign powers — the East India Companies could
wage war, sign treaties, coin money, administer territory, and keep standing armies. A private
profit-seeking corporation became, in effect, a state: the English East India Company conquered
and ruled the Indian subcontinent for a century before the British Crown took over. This is a new
kind of archy — power exercised not by a king or a class of nobles but by a legal abstraction
optimized for accumulation, dispersing moral responsibility across thousands of distant
shareholders who simply received a dividend. We will meet its lineal descendant, the modern
transnational corporation, in Era 8.

Mercantilism and the logic of extraction
The governing economic doctrine, mercantilism, held that national wealth was finite (measured
in bullion) and that the state should maximize its share — promoting exports, hoarding silver
and gold, and above all acquiring colonies as captive sources of raw materials and captive
markets for finished goods. The colony existed for the metropole; its economy was deliberately
structured to extract value outward. This is the tributary logic of Era 5 rescaled to the planet: the
colony stands to the imperial center as the peasant stood to the lord, producing a surplus
pumped upward and outward. The silver of Potosí — mined under the brutal mita forced-labor
draft imposed on Andean communities, itself a colonial perversion of an older Inca labor
obligation — flowed to Spain and on to China, financing European power with American
minerals dug by coerced American hands.
Racialized chattel slavery: the catastrophe and its novelty
At the center of the Atlantic system sat the largest forced migration in human history and one of
its greatest crimes: the transatlantic slave trade, which tore an estimated twelve million or
more Africans from their homes and shipped them across the Atlantic — perhaps one and a half
to two million dying in the Middle Passage alone — to labor and die on the plantations of the
Americas. The sugar, tobacco, coffee, and cotton that built European and American wealth were
grown by enslaved people worked, on the sugar islands especially, to death and replaced by
fresh purchases.
It is essential to see what was new here, because slavery was ancient. Three features made
Atlantic slavery distinct and, in its specific cruelty, unprecedented.
First, its scale and economic centrality — this was slavery as the engine of a transcontinental
capitalist economy, integrated with global finance, insurance, shipping, and manufacturing, not a
domestic or incidental institution but the productive heart of the whole Atlantic system.
Second, its heritable, perpetual, total chattel character — the enslaved and all their
descendants forever were property, with virtually no path to freedom and no recognized
personhood, a more absolute and more permanent bondage than the debt-slavery of
Mesopotamia (which the jubilee released) or even Roman slavery (where manumission was
common and a freedman's son could be a citizen).
Third, and most consequentially for everything after, its justification by race. Earlier slavery
was largely the fortune of war or debt — anyone might fall into it, and it carried no theory that
the enslaved were a different kind of human. Atlantic slavery, confronting the awkward
contradiction between Christian universalism, emerging Enlightenment talk of natural rights, and
the brute fact of chattel bondage, generated a monstrous resolution: the invention of race. The
idea that humanity divides into biologically distinct ranked groups, that Africans were a naturally
inferior kind suited to servitude, was elaborated precisely to square the circle — to let people
who proclaimed liberty and human dignity hold other human beings as property. Race is, in

-- 20 of 74 --

significant part, a product of slavery rather than its cause — an ideology built to justify an
economic system, then outliving it by centuries. This is Aristotle's natural-slavery doctrine (Era
4) reborn in pseudo-biological form and welded to skin color, and it became the most poisonous
and durable hierarchy-justifying idea of the modern world, structuring societies long after the
slavery that birthed it was abolished.
The great inversion: the counter-current finally crosses the abolition horizon
And here, for the first time in this entire history, the counter-current does something genuinely
new. Recall that classical antiquity could not even imagine abolishing slavery (Era 4) — the
institution was below the horizon of the thinkable. In the later eighteenth century that horizon
opened. A broad abolition movement arose — Quakers and other dissenting Christians,
formerly enslaved writers and activists like Olaudah Equiano whose own testimony pierced the
comfortable abstraction, Enlightenment philosophers turning natural-rights logic against the
slavery their contemporaries practiced — and for the first time a mass movement declared that
slavery was not a regrettable necessity or a natural fact but an abomination to be ended
everywhere.
It did not succeed by moral argument alone, and honesty requires saying so. The single
greatest blow against Atlantic slavery was struck by the enslaved themselves: the Haitian
Revolution (1791–1804), in which the enslaved people of the richest plantation colony on earth
rose, defeated the armies of France, Spain, and Britain, and founded a free state — the only
instance in history of a slave revolt creating a nation. Haiti made vivid what Spartacus could not:
that the enslaved could not merely flee but win, and that the natural-hierarchy ideology was a lie
the dominated could disprove with their own hands. The combination of slave resistance,
abolitionist mobilization, shifting economic interest, and revolutionary ideology drove the long
nineteenth-century dismantling of legal slavery across the Atlantic world — Britain's trade ban
(1807) and emancipation (1833), the U.S. Civil War (1865), Brazil last in the Americas (1888).
This is the most important single development in the modern history of archy, and it deserves to
be marked as such. After at least five thousand years in which the great hierarchies were
periodically relieved (the jubilee) or fled (Spartacus, the maroon communities) but never
abolished in principle, a form of bondage as old as civilization was, across roughly a century,
declared illegitimate and largely dismantled. The leveling impulse of Era 1 — never
extinguished, surfacing in every peasant revolt — at last achieved escape velocity, generating
not just relief but the idea that a whole category of domination must end. That idea, once
loosed, would not stay confined to slavery; it would turn next on the wage-labor system that the
very same era was building, and on every other inherited hierarchy of rank, race, and sex.
What this era establishes
The Atlantic centuries globalize archy and give it two of its most consequential modern
instruments: the corporation — immortal, impersonal, accountability-dispersing, sometimes
sovereign, optimized purely for accumulation — and race — a hierarchy of human kinds
invented to justify a slavery more total than any before it. Power now operates at planetary scale

-- 21 of 74 --

through legal abstractions and pseudo-biological ideology. But the same era opens, for the first
time in the species' history, the abolition horizon: the genuinely novel idea, proven on the
battlefield of Haiti and in the abolitionist movement, that an entire ancient form of domination
can be not merely relieved or escaped but ended. The next era will be defined by the collision
between an industrial capitalism inheriting these instruments and a counter-current that has
finally learned it can win.
Era 7 — Industrial capitalism
c. 1800 – 1970: wage labor, the factory, the nation-state, and the century the
counter-current won its greatest victories
Industrialization transformed the form of archy more profoundly than anything since the first
states, and it did so while the abolition horizon opened in Era 6 was widening into a general
assault on inherited hierarchy of every kind. This era is therefore a genuine collision — the most
concentrated machinery of extraction yet built, met by the most powerful organized resistance
yet mounted. To tell it as either triumph or tragedy is to miss it; it is both at once, and the tension
between them is the point.
The new form of domination: free labor, unfree conditions
The defining novelty is wage labor as the dominant relation of production, and its novelty is
genuinely puzzling at first. The industrial worker was, unlike the slave or the serf, legally free —
free to quit, to move, to sell his labor to whomever he chose, owning himself entirely. The chains
of Parts 4 through 6 — chattel ownership, binding to the soil — were gone. So where is the
archy?
It lies in the structure the political economists of the age, and Marx most penetratingly, exposed.
The worker is free in person but separated from the means of production — he owns no land,
no workshop, no machine, nothing he can live from except his capacity to work. The factory, the
tools, the raw materials are owned by the capitalist. So the worker's "freedom" is the freedom to
sell his labor or starve: he must take employment on whatever terms are offered, because the
alternative is destitution. This is the granary coercion of Era 2 in its purest and most abstract
form — control of the means of subsistence translating into control of the people who lack them
— but now achieved without legal bondage, through the seemingly neutral mechanism of a
voluntary contract between formally equal parties. The domination is real but it is masked by the
form of free exchange, which is precisely what makes it so durable and so hard to attack: no
one owns the worker, no law binds him, and yet the structural compulsion to submit is nearly as
total as serfdom. Where the feudal lord said you may not leave, the factory says you may leave
whenever you like — and go where?

The factory itself was a new technology of control as much as of production. It gathered
workers under one roof, under the discipline of the clock, the bell, the assembly line, and the
foreman's eye, subordinating the rhythms of the human body to the tireless rhythm of the
machine. The craftsman who had owned his tools and set his own pace became the operative
who served a machine he did not own at a pace he did not set. A century later this would be
intensified into a science: Taylorism, "scientific management," which broke work into timed,
deskilled fragments and concentrated all knowledge and decision in management — stripping
the worker of autonomy and judgment, reducing him as far as possible to an interchangeable,
controllable component. The deskilling is itself a strategy of power: a worker who knows only
one fragment is easily replaced and hard to organize.
The nation-state: archy's modern container
The political container for all this was the modern nation-state, which over the nineteenth and
twentieth centuries became vastly more capable than any previous form of rule — and that
growing capacity cut, as always, both ways. The state acquired the ability to count, track,
school, conscript, tax, and police its population with unprecedented thoroughness: the census,
the passport, compulsory mass education, the standing bureaucracy, the police force, eventually
the fingerprint and the identity card. The historian and anthropologist James C. Scott described
this as the state's drive to make society "legible" — to reorganize a messy, illegible human
world into standardized, countable, controllable units (fixed surnames, standardized measures,
gridded cities, cadastral maps) that a central authority can see and therefore govern. Legibility is
the ledger of Era 3 grown to encompass the entire population: the state that can see you can
tax, conscript, and discipline you. The same capacities, turned to their darkest extreme in the
twentieth century, enabled totalitarian control and industrialized genocide — the modern state's
power to know and organize its people is also the power that made the Holocaust
administratively possible.
And nationalism supplied the legitimating ideology, the heir to the divine charter and the three
orders. It recast the population not as subjects of a dynasty but as a people, a nation with a
shared identity and destiny, binding the individual to the state through identity and belonging
rather than through naked coercion — and, like classical citizenship (Era 4), defining the nation
always against outsiders, so that belonging and exclusion were again two sides of one coin.
Nationalism could be emancipatory (it powered anti-colonial liberation) and murderous (it
powered the World Wars and ethnic cleansing), often at once.
The counter-current at full force: the century of organized resistance
Here is where this era earns its place as the great collision, because the same industrial
conditions that concentrated workers in factories and cities also concentrated their power to
resist, and the abolition horizon of Era 6 widened into a general assault on hierarchy. For the
first time since the foragers, the leveling impulse operated not as a doomed peasant revolt or a
desperate slave flight but as sustained, organized, partly successful mass movements that
permanently altered the structure of power. Three great currents ran together.

The labor movement turned the workers' one structural asset — their numbers and their
indispensability — into organized force. Individually the worker faced the employer as a
powerless supplicant; combined in a trade union, withholding labor collectively through the
strike, workers discovered they could bargain. This is, in a deep sense, the reverse dominance
hierarchy of Era 1 reinvented for industrial conditions: the many combining to check the power
of the few, the foragers' collective leveling reborn as the union and the strike. Against ferocious
resistance — troops fired on strikers, organizers were jailed and killed, unions were long illegal
as criminal conspiracies — the movement won across decades the things now so normalized
we forget they were fought for and are reversible: limits on the working day, the weekend, the
abolition of child labor, workplace safety law, the minimum wage, the right to organize itself.
Where labor was scarce or organized it could extract these concessions; where abundant or
suppressed it could not — the labor-leverage lesson of Era 5's Black Death, now made the
conscious basis of strategy.
The extension of the franchise turned the political hierarchy of citizenship (Era 4) into a terrain
of struggle. The vote, once the jealously guarded privilege of propertied men, was prised open
step by grudging step — to working men, then through the long and often violent suffrage
movements to women, and through the civil rights struggles to racial minorities excluded in law
or in fact. Each extension was resisted as the end of civilization and each, once won, became
unthinkable to reverse. Formal political equality — one person, one vote — is a historically
astonishing achievement set against the five thousand years of graded belonging that preceded
it, even where its substance remained constrained by inequalities of wealth and power that
voting alone could not touch.
And the broad emancipatory movements — the completion of abolition and the long
subsequent struggle against its racial afterlife; the women's movement confronting the
patriarchy that is the oldest archy of all, older than the state, predating every structure in this
document and woven through all of them; the anti-colonial movements that after 1945
dismantled the formal empires of Era 6 and reclaimed self-rule for most of humanity — together
represent the leveling impulse turning on every inherited hierarchy of rank, race, sex, and
empire at once. The horizon that opened with the idea that slavery could be abolished kept
widening until, in principle, no inherited domination was any longer beyond question.
The institutional settlement that emerged in the mid-twentieth-century West — the
social-democratic compromise, the welfare state, mass unionization, progressive taxation,
the partial decommodification of health, education, and old age — represented the high-water
mark of this counter-current: not the abolition of capitalist archy but its most thorough taming,
the closest the modern world came to rebuilding, at the scale of the nation-state, something of
the forager principle that no one should be allowed to fall too low or rise too far above the rest. It
was, like the jubilee (Era 3), a managed relief of the system's pressures rather than a
dismantling of the machine — but it was real, and its partial unwinding is the subject of the final
part.

What this era establishes
Industrial capitalism produces a masked form of domination — real coercion through the means
of subsistence, hidden behind the free and equal labor contract — and a vastly more capable
nation-state that can see, count, and control its population as never before. But it is also the era
in which the counter-current, after millennia as a losing force, mounts sustained and partly
victorious mass resistance: the union and the strike reinventing collective leveling, the franchise
turning citizenship into contested ground, the great emancipations turning on inherited hierarchy
of every kind, and the welfare state achieving the most thorough taming of archy the large-scale
world has seen. The story here is genuinely double — concentration and resistance, the ledger
and the strike, advancing together. The final part asks what happened when, after about 1970,
the concentrating forces found new instruments and the taming forces went into retreat.
Era 8 — The present
c. 1970 – now: financialization, globalization, algorithmic management, data as surplus
— and the question "novel, or familiar?"
The final part is the hardest, because we lack the distance that turns events into history, and
because the present is precisely where the inevitability and "novel-or-familiar" threads must be
paid off rather than admired from afar. The organizing claim is this: since roughly 1970 the
taming of archy achieved by the mid-century social-democratic settlement (Era 7) has
substantially unwound, while the concentrating forces have acquired genuinely new
instruments. Whether those instruments represent something unprecedented or merely the
ancient mechanisms in new clothing is the question that gives this era its character — and the
most useful answer, examined below, is both: the form is novel, the underlying logic is among
the oldest in this document.
The turn: from taming back toward concentration
Around the 1970s the postwar arrangement began to reverse across much of the world: the
partial decommodification of life retreated, unions were weakened by law and by the offshoring
of the industrial jobs that had been their base, top tax rates fell, capital was deregulated and
freed to move across borders, and the doctrine that markets should govern more and states
less became orthodoxy. The measurable result is a return of wealth and income concentration
toward levels not seen since before the Era 7 counter-current — the great mid-century
compression of inequality has been substantially reversed in many countries.
The current numbers make the scale concrete and are worth stating plainly. On average, a
person in the richest 1% owns thousands of times more wealth than someone in the bottom
50%, and the poorest half of humanity holds just over half of one percent of the world's wealth,
while the richest 1% own close to 44%. The World Inequality Report 2026 found that the top

10% of the world's income earners earn more than the other 90% combined. Billionaire wealth
reached roughly $18.3 trillion in 2025, its highest level in history, having risen by around 81%
since 2020, even as one in four people do not regularly have enough to eat and nearly half the
world's population lives in poverty. This is not a side-effect to be noted and set aside; it is the
headline structural fact of the era, the unwinding of the only large-scale taming of archy the
modern world ever achieved.
Financialization: power moves upstream from production to ownership
The first novel-seeming mechanism is financialization — the growing dominance of finance, of
the ownership and trading of claims on wealth, over the actual production of goods and
services. Profit increasingly accrues not to those who make things but to those who own the
financial claims: shares, debt, derivatives, real estate, intellectual property. This is, in one light,
the purest distillation of the corporation of Era 6 and the abstract money of Era 4 — power as
ownership of liquid, accumulable claims, now grown to govern the whole economy. The
asset-owner extracts a flow of rent, interest, and capital gains from the productive activity of
others without necessarily organizing or contributing to that activity at all.
The deepest continuity here is debt, the master-mechanism first met in Era 3. Household,
student, medical, consumer, and sovereign debt bind populations and whole nations into
obligation, and debt operates today exactly as it did in Mesopotamia: a temporary shortfall
converts, through interest, into a durable claim on the debtor's future labor and assets, and it
moralizes the relationship so the debtor feels the obligation as personal failing rather than
structural design. The crucial difference from Era 3 is the absence of the jubilee. The ancient
kings periodically wiped the slate clean to prevent debt from hollowing out their societies; the
modern system has, for the most part, no comparable institutional reset (sovereign-debt crises
and the occasional bankruptcy aside), and the question of whether a debt jubilee is needed has
returned to serious discussion precisely because the ancient pressure the jubilee was designed
to relieve has returned with it.
Globalization: the spatial reorganization of extraction
Globalization reorganized archy across space. Production was distributed along global supply
chains to wherever labor was cheapest and least protected, which let capital escape the
bargained settlement of Era 7: a factory that can relocate to a lower-wage jurisdiction holds a
structural threat over any workforce that organizes, neutralizing the strike weapon that won the
twentieth century's gains. This is, at bottom, the labor-leverage principle of Era 5's Black Death
running in reverse — where a labor shortage once empowered the medieval peasant, the global
integration of a vast low-wage labor supply weakened labor everywhere by making it abundant
and substitutable. The colonial extraction-pattern of Era 6 also persists in modified form: despite
being home to 79 percent of the world's population, Global South countries own just 31 percent
of global wealth. The relation of metropole to colony has become the relation of capital-owning
core to labor-supplying periphery — the tributary logic of Era 5 globalized, with value still
pumped from periphery toward center.

Algorithmic management: the foreman becomes code
Here is the mechanism that feels most genuinely new, and it rewards close examination
because it is where the "novel or familiar?" question bites hardest. In the gig economy and
increasingly in warehouses, call centers, and conventional workplaces, the managerial functions
once performed by human supervisors are now performed by software. On platforms like the
food-delivery and ride-hailing services, algorithms operating on data captured from workers' own
smartphones determine the allocation of work, the pay, the discipline, and sometimes even the
termination of the worker.
Scholars have noticed the deep historical rhyme, and it is exact. Algorithmic management is
argued to constitute a contemporary form of scientific management — Taylorism reborn — in
which jobs are fragmented into simple repetitive tasks, the labor process is tightly controlled for
maximum efficiency, and workers who underperform can be easily identified and replaced. The
Taylorism of Era 7 is back, but automated, continuous, and vastly more granular: where the
human foreman could watch only intermittently, the algorithm watches always. The irony has
been noted that even as penal systems have largely abandoned Panopticon-based prison
designs on ethical grounds, modern workplaces have adopted similar principles of constant
observation under the banner of efficiency and optimization.
So is it novel or familiar? Both, and the structure of the "both" is the lesson. The logic is ancient
— control through surveillance, deskilling, and the threat of replacement is Taylorism (Era 7),
and behind Taylorism the factory bell, and behind the bell the foreman's eye, and behind that the
overseer. The form is new in three ways that matter. The surveillance is now total and
continuous rather than intermittent. The authority is depersonalized into code, so there is no
foreman to argue with, no human whose judgment can be appealed to or whose conscience can
be reached — a worker "disciplined in real-time" by an algorithm faces a wall, not a person, the
corporation's dispersal of responsibility (Era 6) carried to its endpoint where no human at all is
visibly responsible for the domination. And the worker is often legally classified as an
independent contractor rather than an employee, which strips away the very protections the Era
7 counter-current won — no minimum wage, no collective bargaining, no safety net — while the
control exerted is as tight as any factory's or tighter. This last move is the era's signature trick: it
combines the freedom rhetoric of the wage contract (Era 7) — you're your own boss, you
choose your hours — with a degree of control that the wage contract's critics would have
recognized instantly, achieving the substance of subordination while disclaiming its form. It is the
masking of domination behind free exchange, perfected.
The leveling counter-current is responding, as it always has, and the response confirms the
pattern. Gig workers have developed "workaround strategies" to cope with and regain control
under platform algorithms, and have built external alliances and digital tools to resist and protect
themselves from algorithmic control and surveillance — a repertoire scholars classify as
manipulation, subversion, and disruption. This is the reverse dominance hierarchy of Era 1 and

the union of Era 7 reaching for new tools against a new form of the old domination — collective
resistance reinventing itself once more, now against an adversary made of code.
Data as surplus: a candidate for the genuinely new
If anything in the present is truly unprecedented, it may be this. The era's defining resource is
data, and the relation built on it — variously called surveillance capitalism — has a structure
with no exact precedent in the preceding seven parts. Human behavior itself, recorded as data
exhaust from everything we do online and increasingly offline, becomes raw material: captured,
often without meaningful consent or awareness, refined by machine learning into predictions
about what we will do, and sold — chiefly, so far, to those who want to influence our purchasing
and our voting.
Why might this be genuinely novel rather than another old mechanism in new dress? Every prior
form of extraction in this document took something external — the harvest, the labor of the body,
the silver in the ground, the hours at the machine. Data extraction takes the record of behavior
itself, including behavior we don't experience as labor or as a transaction at all, and turns the
prediction and modification of behavior into the product. The surplus being accumulated is, in a
sense, a slice of human experience and autonomy. It concentrates in a handful of firms an
asymmetry of knowledge-about-people unlike anything a god-king or a factory owner ever
possessed: they know an enormous amount about each of us, and we know almost nothing
about their systems — an information asymmetry that is itself a profound and new form of
power, the ledger of Era 3 grown to encompass not just what we owe but what we are likely to
do.
Yet even here the familiar reasserts itself, and intellectual honesty requires holding both. The
underlying drive — to know the population in order to govern and extract from it — is exactly the
state's drive toward "legibility" (Era 7) and behind it the ledger of Era 3. Data is the Uruk grain
tablet's most distant descendant: a technology for seeing people so as to extract from them.
What is new is the totality, the granularity, the privatization (this legibility belongs to corporations
as much as states), and above all the turn from recording the past to predicting and shaping the
future. So the most defensible verdict on the whole era is the one the question itself invites: the
present is the oldest mechanisms — extraction, debt, surveillance, the masking of coercion as
free exchange, the dispersal of responsibility — running on radically new infrastructure that
changes their scale, their speed, their totality, and in the single case of behavioral data, arguably
their kind.
What this era establishes
The present is the unwinding of the twentieth century's taming of archy and the re-concentration
of wealth and power toward pre-counter-current levels, achieved through financialization (power
as ownership of liquid claims, with debt as the master-mechanism and no jubilee), globalization
(extraction reorganized across space, labor weakened by its own global abundance),
algorithmic management (Taylorist control perfected — total, continuous, depersonalized into
code, and combined with the stripping-away of hard-won protections), and data extraction (the

Synthesis: the mechanisms, the verdict, and the counter-current
Having walked the eight eras, we can now extract what was promised at the outset — not a
single thesis but a map of the recurring devices by which the power of one human over another
is generated, and an honest answer to the two framing questions.
The recurring mechanisms of archy
Across thirty thousand years, the same small set of devices reappears in era after era, each
time in new dress. Naming them together makes the structure visible.
Control of the means of subsistence. The oldest and deepest mechanism. The forager
sharing economy was engineered to deny anyone this power; the Neolithic granary first supplied
it; the temple-state institutionalized it; industrial capitalism abstracted it into the wage relation
("work or starve"); the present extends it through debt and the platform. Whoever controls what
others need to live can command them, and almost every form of archy reduces, at bottom, to
some version of this.
Debt. The master-mechanism, because it achieves domination with the victim's consent and
self-blame. It appears fully formed in Mesopotamia, is periodically reset by the jubilee, is
supercharged by coinage, runs through colonial and industrial bondage, and dominates the
financialized present — now without the jubilee. Debt is the cheapest tool of power because it
recruits the debtor's own sense of obligation.
The ledger. The technology of memory and legibility. Writing was invented to track what people
owe; the state's power has always rested on its ability to see its population (census, map,
surname, passport); the present extends this to the total, predictive surveillance of data
extraction. To be seen by power is to be governable by it.
Sacralization and naturalization. The solution to legitimacy — the chronic problem that the
many outnumber the few. The god-king embodies the cosmos; the three orders and Confucian
duty make hierarchy harmonious; caste makes it karmic; Aristotle and race make it natural and
inborn. The most durable hierarchies are those that convince the dominated their subordination
reflects the order of the universe or their own essential nature.
The dispersal of responsibility. The corporation (Era 6) made power impersonal and immortal,
separating those who own from those who manage from those who suffer, so that no individual

-- 29 of 74 --

need feel responsible for what the structure does. Algorithmic management (Era 8) carries this
to its endpoint, dissolving the responsible human entirely into code. Diffusing accountability is
itself a technology of power.
The masking of coercion as free exchange. The subtlest mechanism, perfected late. The
wage contract (Era 7) achieves real subordination through a form that looks like a free
agreement between equals; the gig platform (Era 8) perfects the trick, combining the rhetoric of
autonomy with the substance of total control. Domination is most stable when it does not look
like domination.
The abolition of exit. Running beneath all of these is the loss of the forager's ultimate check —
the right to walk away. Sedentism took it (Era 2), serfdom abolished it by binding labor to land
(Era 5), the wage system replaced it with "leave and go where?" (Era 7), and globalization
turned it against labor by giving capital the mobility that workers lack (Era 8). Watch the right to
leave, and you can read the rise of archy in any era.

oppressions but a sequence of removed defenses, each link stripping away one of the things
that kept foragers equal. The links are cumulative rather than strictly chronological: once a link
is added it rarely disappears, so a modern worker is subject to nearly all of them at once.

The verdict on inevitability
The strong inevitability thesis — agriculture made hierarchy automatic — fails. Çatalhöyük had
every necessary condition and stayed flat for a millennium (Era 2); the Indus civilization built
cities without god-kings (Era 3); Neolithic communities built inequality and then dismantled it;
the deep Palaeolithic may have moved consciously between political modes (Era 1). The ratchet
did not lock everywhere it could have, and it sometimes ran backwards. Hierarchy is a recurrent
human possibility, reached by genuinely different roads (chattel slavery in Rome, bureaucratic
duty in Han China, caste in India), not a law of nature.
But the weak version holds, and it is sobering. The Neolithic and after did supply necessary
conditions — storable surplus, heritable estates, reduced exit, the death of demand-sharing —
that made durable archy available as it had never been, and that progressively defeated the
leveling mechanisms one by one. The dice were loaded even though they were not thrown for
us. The honest verdict is therefore neither "always coming" nor "single wrong turn," but: humans
build hierarchy and dismantle it repeatedly, and it locks in under identifiable conditions —
concentrated storable defensible resources, plus the loss of exit — which the modern world has
supplied at ever greater scale. Inequality is neither natural nor accidental; it is conditional and
constructed, which means it is also, in principle, reversible — a fact the next thread makes
concrete.
The through-line: the counter-current never died
The single most important thread running the length of this document is that the leveling impulse
of Era 1 was never extinguished. It was defeated, masked, sacralized away, outscaled — but it
surfaced in every era, and over time it learned to win.
It runs from the foragers who ridiculed the boaster and executed the tyrant (Era 1); through the
megalithic communal tombs refusing the individuating monument (Era 2); through the jubilee
that periodically wiped the slate clean (Era 3); through the Stoic insistence on inner freedom and
the revolt of Spartacus (Era 4); through the peasant risings asking "when Adam delved and Eve
span, who was then the gentleman?" (Era 5); through the abolition movement and the Haitian

Revolution that first crossed the abolition horizon — the genuinely new idea that an entire
ancient form of domination could be ended, not merely relieved or fled (Era 6); through the
unions, the strike, the franchise, the great emancipations, and the welfare state that achieved
the most thorough taming of archy the large-scale world has seen (Era 7); and into the present,
where gig workers reinvent collective resistance against management-by-algorithm (Era 8).

abolition movement, the union, and the universal franchise. We are not by nature levelers or by
nature climbers. We are the animal that does both, and which one prevails is decided — as it
was decided around every forager campfire that mocked a man for boasting about his kill — by
institutions, conditions, and the choices of the many about how much power they will allow the
few. The history of archy is, read correctly, also the history of its perennial contestation. The
story is not over, and on the evidence of thirty thousand years, it was never going to be.

How to read this document, and how much to trust the numbers
Part I traced the mechanisms by which the power of one human over another was built and
dismantled across eight eras. This part does something narrower and more concrete: it asks, for
each of those same eras, how big were the groups, how were they structured, what did
ordinary people contribute and keep, how much did the top accumulate, who pushed
back, and on what grounds did rulers claim the right to rule. Where a number exists, it
appears. Where a number is an estimate, it is marked as such. Where there is no honest
number, the text says so rather than inventing one.
A word on the central measure. Most quantified inequality here uses the Gini coefficient, a
scale from 0 (everyone holds exactly the same) to 1 (one household holds everything). For
ancient societies with no tax records, archaeologists cannot measure income, so they use a
clever proxy: the variation in house sizes within a settlement. If every dwelling is nearly the
same size, the Gini is low; if a few mansions tower over many hovels, it is high. The single most

-- 32 of 74 --

useful body of work here is Kohler, Smith, and colleagues' 2017 study in Nature, which
calculated house-size Ginis for 63 archaeological sites across four continents and 11,000 years.
Its headline series — which recurs throughout this document — runs roughly as follows:
hunter-gatherers a median Gini of about 0.17, small-scale horticulturalists about 0.27, larger
agricultural societies about 0.35, and the most unequal ancient Old World societies reaching
about 0.59, against a modern United States figure variously pegged at 0.80 to 0.85. Keep that
arc in mind; it is the quantitative spine of everything below.
Three honesty caveats apply throughout. First, certainty degrades as we go back. A Roman
census figure and a modern Gini are real measurements; a Palaeolithic "group size" is an
inference from living foragers and a handful of sites. Each section flags its confidence. Second,
house-size Gini is a proxy, not the thing itself — it captures durable wealth, not income,
slaves, or political power, and it can understate inequality in societies where the elite's
advantage lay in people owned or offices held rather than floor space. Third, every era
contained enormous internal variation; the figures are central tendencies, not universal laws,
and the counter-cases (a flat city here, a steep chiefdom there) matter as much as the
averages.

- [measured] — based on real records or direct archaeological measurement; the most

- [estimated] — a scholarly reconstruction or model, defensible but with real error bars.

Era 1 — Forager egalitarianism
Deep prehistory – c. 12,000 BCE
Group size. The basic foraging unit, the band or "overnight camp," averaged roughly 25
people, commonly ranging from about 25 to 50 [inferred, from living hunter-gatherers]. Bands
were nested in larger but more diffuse layers: a community or "cultural lineage" of perhaps
100–230 people (the band-of-bands that met, intermarried, and shared a dialect), and a tribe or
regional pool of perhaps 500–2,500 who shared a language and met only periodically [inferred].
The often-cited "Dunbar's number" of ~150 names the community layer, not the camp; recent
fieldwork finds local bands averaging closer to 30, with wide variation (one survey found group
sizes from 42 to over 440), so the round numbers should be held loosely [contested]. The

-- 33 of 74 --

decisive structural fact is that the unit you lived with day to day was small enough that everyone
knew everyone, and membership was fluid — you could leave one band and join another.
Structure and roles. Essentially flat. Division of labor ran mainly by age and sex (a
generalization with many exceptions), not by class. Influence accrued to skilled hunters, wise
elders, gifted mediators and storytellers, but it was non-coercive and non-heritable — a
respected hunter's son inherited no office. There were no full-time specialists, no standing
authority, no one who did not also forage.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. Almost everything acquired was shared, especially
large game, through obligatory distribution and "demand sharing" (anyone may ask, refusal is
shameful). What people kept was personal: tools, ornaments, clothing — what one could carry.
What anyone accumulated was close to nil, because mobility punishes hoarding and the
leveling ethic forbids it. This is why the forager Gini sits near the floor of the human range: a
median of about 0.17 [measured, for sampled sites], the lowest of any society type in the
Kohler series. The residual 0.17 is real but reflects differences in portable goods and skill, not
the institutional, heritable wealth gap of later eras.
Counter-currents (here, the main current). This is the one era where leveling is not the
counter-current but the dominant force. The toolkit — ridicule of boasters, "insulting the meat,"
demand sharing, the freedom to walk away, and in the extreme the ostracism or killing of a
would-be tyrant — constitutes Christopher Boehm's reverse dominance hierarchy: the many
actively holding down any one who tries to rise. Archy is the deviation that has to fight this.
Claim to rule. None, in the institutional sense. To the extent anyone "led," the claim was
competence and consent in the moment — people followed a good hunter on a hunt and a
good mediator in a dispute, and stopped following when the task ended. There was no standing
claim, no office, no inheritance, no appeal to gods or birth.
Era 2 — The Neolithic threshold
c. 12,000 – 4,000 BCE
Group size. Settlement allowed unprecedented aggregation. Early villages ran from dozens to
a few hundred; the great proto-town of Çatalhöyük held on the order of 3,500–8,000 people
at its peak [estimated], an almost unimaginable crowd by forager standards, packed into
clustered mudbrick houses. The pre-agricultural ceremonial site of Göbekli Tepe drew labor from
a wide region for monumental building [estimated]. The ceiling on group size was rising by one
to two orders of magnitude.

Structure and roles. Contested and variable — which is the era's whole lesson. Some
communities developed emergent rank; others, strikingly, did not. Çatalhöyük shows
near-uniform houses with no palace, no elite quarter, and no rich-versus-poor housing for
roughly a thousand years, implying a powerful ideology of household equivalence [measured,
from architecture; interpretation contested]. Specialization (potters, weavers, builders) was
emerging but had not yet hardened into class.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. The novelty is the storable, heritable surplus:
grain in the granary, animals in the herd, a cleared field passing to one's children. For the first
time, what a household kept and accumulated could exceed what it immediately consumed and
could be transmitted across generations. Sharing did not vanish, but demand-sharing could not
survive a harvest one household grew and another did not. Quantitatively, the move off the
forager floor is clear: small-scale horticulturalists show a median Gini of about 0.27, and larger
agricultural communities about 0.35 [measured, for sampled sites] — a near-doubling from the
forager 0.17. But the variance is the point: Çatalhöyük and the Carpathian Basin communities
stayed remarkably flat for millennia (one Science Advances study found Carpathian societies
resisting inequality for ~5,000 years) [measured], proving the higher median was a tendency,
not a law.
Counter-currents. Now genuinely a counter-current, and visibly active. The great communal
megalithic tombs — long barrows and passage graves pooling many anonymous bodies —
assert the collective over any individual, the leveling ethos rendered in stone. Some
communities built emergent hierarchy and then dismantled it, the ratchet running backwards.
The forager leveling instinct had not died; it was fighting on harder terrain.
Claim to rule. Still thin and emergent. Where leaders arose, the claim rested on the "big man"
/ aggrandizer pathway: generosity that creates debt. A lavish feast, funded from surplus, put
guests under an obligation discharged through loyalty and labor — prestige convertible, for the
first time, into a following. The claim was not yet birth or gods but beneficence weaponized: I
feed you, therefore you owe me.
Era 3 — The first states and the god-kings
c. 4,000 – 800 BCE
Group size. The state shatters the old ceilings. Early Uruk may have held 25,000–50,000
people; by the later third and second millennia some Mesopotamian and Egyptian cities and
their hinterlands governed hundreds of thousands, and the largest territorial states
approached or exceeded a million subjects [estimated]. The face-to-face band of 25 is now a
rounding error inside a polity of a million strangers — and ruling strangers who cannot all know
each other is precisely the problem the new tools solve.

-- 35 of 74 --

Structure and roles. The first true class society with full-time specialists who do not farm: a
ruler and royal household; a priesthood managing the temple estates; scribes and bureaucrats
running the ledgers; soldiers; artisans; a mass of peasant cultivators; and, at the bottom,
debt-bondsmen and slaves. The defining innovation is the standing office that outlives its holder
— power is now institutional, not merely personal.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. Peasants contributed a share of harvest as
tax/tribute and a season of corvée (compulsory labor on canals, walls, temples, tombs). The
temple-state was the great accumulator: it owned much of the land, ran the central granary,
employed and rationed labor. Private accumulation now ran steeply upward through office, land,
and above all debt — a bad harvest forced a loan at savage interest, default forfeited oxen,
then land, then children, then the debtor himself. The wealth gap widens sharply: ancient Old
World Ginis climb toward their eventual peak of about 0.59 in the most stratified societies
[estimated/measured], though early-state figures sit somewhere between the agricultural 0.35
and that ceiling. House-size Gini almost certainly understates real inequality here, because the
pharaoh's or king's advantage lay in owning people and commanding the state, not merely in
floor area.
Counter-currents. The signature device is the debt jubilee: the Mesopotamian "clean slate"
(andurarum) and the biblical Jubilee, in which non-commercial debts were cancelled,
debt-slaves freed, and forfeited land restored — often proclaimed by a new king. Crucially this
was managed relief from above, designed to stop debt from hollowing out the taxpaying,
soldiering population, not a popular liberation. The leveling impulse survives, but now as royal
policy venting the system's worst pressure rather than as the band restraining a boaster. The
era's deepest counter-evidence is structural: the Indus Valley civilization ran large planned
cities for centuries with no convincing palaces, royal tombs, or god-king iconography
[measured/contested] — complexity without the king.
Claim to rule. The great invention: divinity. The Egyptian pharaoh was a god, the living Horus,
guarantor of ma'at (cosmic order); the Mesopotamian king ruled as the god's chosen steward,
the city literally owned by its deity. To disobey was not crime but sacrilege — the unmaking of
the world. China's later Mandate of Heaven added a revolutionary clause: Heaven grants rule
to the just and withdraws it from the cruel, making rule conditional and supplying a ready
justification for overthrowing a tyrant. The claim moves from "I feed you" (Era 2) to "the cosmos
commands you obey me."

Era 4 — Classical empires and slavery
c. 800 BCE – 500 CE
Group size. Imperial scale. The Roman Empire at its height governed perhaps 60–70 million
people [estimated]; Han China, contemporaneously, recorded a census of roughly 57–60
million [measured, Han census of 2 CE — remarkably, an actual head-count]. The city of Rome
itself approached one million inhabitants [estimated]. Polities now spanned continents and
dozens of subject peoples.
Structure and roles. Steep, legally codified, and — crucially — graded rather than binary.
Rome ran a ladder of statuses: senators and equestrians at the top, then citizens, Latins,
freedmen, provincial subjects, and at the base a vast enslaved population. Athens drew a hard
line between citizen, metic (resident foreigner), and slave. Han China governed through a
bureaucracy of text-trained officials rather than a slave/free divide, legitimated by
reciprocal-but-unequal Confucian duties. Same outcome — the few over the many at imperial
scale — by different roads.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. This is the era of mass chattel slavery as the
economic foundation. Estimates suggest the enslaved were on the order of a third of the
population of Roman Italy and perhaps 10–15% of the empire overall [estimated/contested],
legally things — bought, sold, worked in mines and on the great estates (latifundia). Free
citizens contributed taxes and military service; the enslaved contributed everything and kept
nothing, not even themselves. Wealth concentrated enormously: a Roman senator's fortune
could exceed that of tens of thousands of ordinary households, and the abstract, accumulable
power of coined money (new in this era) let wealth pile up without limit, tied to no rotting
granary. The classical Gini, where measurable, sits high in the ancient range, near the ~0.59
ceiling in the most stratified cores [estimated].
Counter-currents. Weak, and tellingly so. Slave revolts (Spartacus, 73–71 BCE) sought
escape, not abolition — the very idea of abolishing slavery did not yet exist [the most
important fact in this row]. The Stoics taught inner freedom and a brotherhood of rational beings
(Epictetus, himself once enslaved, held that the master could chain the body but never the will),
yet preached endurance, not overthrow. The leveling impulse had been not merely defeated but
philosophized out of view: Aristotle's doctrine of the "natural slave" recast subordination as the
victim's own inborn nature.
Claim to rule. Plural. In the West, increasingly law, citizenship, and office — the emperor as
supreme magistrate and, later, as a divine or quasi-divine figure (the imperial cult). In Han
China, the Mandate of Heaven plus the claim of meritorious, text-trained competence — rule
justified by learning and the cultivation of virtue. Persia's "king of kings" claimed to rule a

-- 37 of 74 --

tolerated mosaic of peoples by divine favor (Ahura Mazda). The common thread: the claim now
rests on institutions — law, cosmic mandate, office — that outlive any individual ruler.
Era 5 — Feudal and tributary orders
c. 500 – 1450
Group size. Political fragmentation in the European west: after Rome's western collapse,
effective power localized to the manor, the lordship, the small kingdom — units of hundreds to a
few thousand on a given estate, even as nominal kingdoms and the great Asian empires
remained large. The relevant lived unit shrank back toward the village of a few hundred
[estimated]. Elsewhere, large centralized states persisted (Tang and Song China governed tens
of millions; the Islamic caliphates spanned continents) [measured/estimated], so "feudal"
describes a type of relation, not a global scale.
Structure and roles. The defining fact across civilizations is labor bound to land. The
European serf was adscriptus glebae — written into the soil, transferred with the land, owing
labor-days and dues, unable to leave without permission, yet not a saleable chattel. Parallel
systems: Japanese daimyō lords and samurai over land-bound peasants; the Islamic iqṭāʿ
(revocable revenue-grants) and mamlūk military slavery (slaves who could rise to rule); and
India's caste system, the most thoroughly sacralized hierarchy in human history, fixing rank at
birth by religious doctrine of purity, dharma, and rebirth.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. Peasants contributed rent, tithe, dues, and labor
upward to a non-producing elite of warriors and clergy — the tributary mode. A common rough
figure is that the peasant surrendered something like a quarter to a half of output (the church
tithe alone claimed a notional tenth) [estimated, highly variable]. What the peasant kept was
subsistence plus customary use-rights to common land and the lord's protection; what he
accumulated was little, since dues skimmed the surplus. The Church and the nobility were the
great accumulators of land. Wealth inequality stayed high but, in the European west, somewhat
below the most extreme ancient peaks during the early medieval centuries, before climbing
again — the Old World Gini trend resumes its rise toward ~0.59 and beyond as commerce
revives [estimated].
Counter-currents. Vigorous and recurrent. Peasant revolts punctuate the era — the English
Rising of 1381, the German Peasants' War of 1525, French jacqueries, China's Red Turban
risings — often explicitly egalitarian. The radical English question "When Adam delved and Eve
span, who was then the gentleman?" invokes an original equality and brands lords as usurpers.
And a demographic accident, the Black Death (1347–51), killed perhaps a third to half of
Europe and, by making labor scarce, handed surviving peasants real bargaining power: wages
rose, dues fell, and serfdom withered across the western half of the continent [measured] —

-- 38 of 74 --

though in Eastern Europe lords tightened it (the "second serfdom"), a warning against assuming
any single direction.
Claim to rule. Divine right and sacred function. The monarch answerable to God alone (the
direct heir of the god-king charter). The ideology of the three orders — those who pray, those
who fight, those who work — cast hierarchy as a divinely ordained division of mutual service.
Caste pushed this furthest: rank as cosmic and karmic law, subordination deserved across
lifetimes. The claim is now birth plus God: I rule because I was born to it and Heaven wills it.
Era 6 — Mercantilism, colonialism, and the Atlantic system
c. 1450 – 1800
Group size. Power goes global and is exercised at a distance. The relevant unit is no longer the
polity but the empire-plus-trading-network: a chartered company governing millions of
subjects an ocean away. The English East India Company came to rule the Indian
subcontinent's tens of millions; the Atlantic system linked European metropoles, African coasts,
and American plantations into one extractive web spanning continents [estimated].
Structure and roles. Two novel structures dominate. First, the joint-stock chartered company
— an immortal, impersonal accumulator separating ownership (shareholders) from management
(directors) from those who suffer its actions, and granted sovereign powers to wage war, coin
money, and rule territory. Second, racialized chattel slavery: a caste of the permanently,
heritably enslaved, justified by the newly invented ideology of race. Society is now stratified
across continents and color lines simultaneously.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. The enslaved contributed their lives and kept
nothing — the transatlantic trade forcibly shipped an estimated 12.5 million Africans, with
perhaps 1.5–2 million dying in the Middle Passage [estimated, from the Trans-Atlantic Slave
Trade Database — one of the better-quantified atrocities in history]. Their uncompensated labor
grew the sugar, tobacco, and cotton that built metropolitan wealth. The colony, like the medieval
peasant, existed to pump surplus to the center: silver from Potosí (mined under the coerced
mita draft) flowed to Spain and on to China. Accumulation concentrated in merchant elites,
planters, and shareholders; the era opens the long climb of Old World inequality toward and
past its ancient ceiling as global extraction feeds European capital [estimated].
Counter-currents. Here the counter-current does something genuinely new — it crosses the
abolition horizon. For the first time in history a mass movement declared an ancient form of
bondage an abomination to be ended everywhere: Quakers, formerly enslaved activists like
Olaudah Equiano, and Enlightenment natural-rights arguments. And the enslaved proved it
could be won by force: the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) — the only slave revolt in history to

-- 39 of 74 --

found a free state — defeated the armies of France, Spain, and Britain. After five thousand
years of relief (the jubilee) and escape (Spartacus), the idea that a whole category of
domination could be abolished finally existed.
Claim to rule. Transitional and increasingly commercial. Older divine-right claims persisted, but
alongside them came the charter — the legal grant of monopoly and sovereignty to a company
— and the naked claim of conquest and racial superiority: Europeans asserting a right to rule
and enslave grounded in an invented hierarchy of human kinds. The claim is sliding from "God
and birth" toward "ownership, contract, and race."
Era 7 — Industrial capitalism
c. 1800 – 1970
Group size. The nation-state becomes the dominant container, governing tens to hundreds of
millions with unprecedented reach — census, passport, compulsory schooling, conscription,
police. By the mid-twentieth century the largest states governed hundreds of millions; the
planet's population crossed into the billions. The lived economic unit became the factory and
the city — thousands of strangers coordinated under one roof and one clock.
Structure and roles. The central relation is wage labor: workers legally free — owning
themselves, free to quit and move — but separated from the means of production and therefore
compelled to sell their labor "or starve." Society sorts into capital (owners of factories, finance,
land) and labor (those with only their capacity to work), with a growing salaried middle of
managers, clerks, and professionals. Taylorism ("scientific management") deskills work into
timed fragments, concentrating knowledge and control in management.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. Workers contributed labor for a wage; the gap
between the value produced and the wage paid (Marx's surplus value) accumulated as profit to
owners. Early industrial inequality was brutal and rose toward the historic highs — and then,
remarkably, fell. The mid-twentieth-century West saw the "Great Compression": through
unionization, progressive taxation (top marginal rates reached 70–90%+ in the mid-century US
and UK) [measured], and the welfare state, inequality dropped to its lowest modern levels. The
US wealth Gini, pegged around 0.80 later in the century [measured], was meaningfully lower at
mid-century; income inequality in particular compressed hard. This is the most thorough taming
of archy the large-scale world has achieved.
Counter-currents. The strongest in the whole history, and partly victorious. The labor
movement turned workers' numbers into organized force — the union and the strike reinventing
the forager's collective leveling for industrial conditions — winning the eight-hour day, the
weekend, the abolition of child labor, workplace safety, the minimum wage, and the right to

-- 40 of 74 --

organize. The franchise was prised open from propertied men to working men, then (through
often-violent suffrage struggle) to women, then to excluded racial groups. The great
emancipations — completing abolition, the women's movement against the oldest archy of all,
and the post-1945 anti-colonial movements that dismantled the formal empires of Era 6 —
turned the leveling impulse against every inherited hierarchy at once. The horizon that opened
with abolition kept widening until, in principle, no inherited domination was beyond question.
Claim to rule. Shifts decisively toward two modern bases. Politically, popular sovereignty and
the nation — rule justified by representing "the people," legitimated through elections and
national identity rather than God or birth (nationalism the heir to the divine charter, binding the
individual through belonging). Economically, property and contract — the owner's authority
over the workplace grounded in ownership and in the worker's "free" agreement to the wage
bargain. The claim is now "the people authorize me" and "I own it, and you agreed."
Era 8 — The present
c. 1970 – now
Group size. Effectively global and networked. Supply chains, capital flows, and digital
platforms operate across the entire species of ~8 billion. A single platform firm can "manage"
tens of millions of workers and billions of users at once. The lived unit fractures: simultaneously
the whole planet (one integrated economy) and the isolated individual (the gig worker alone with
an app).
Structure and roles. The mid-century taming has substantially unwound. Unions weakened
(often by law and by the offshoring of their industrial base), top tax rates fell, capital was
deregulated and freed to move across borders. New structures: the transnational corporation
(the chartered company's descendant, immortal and accountability-dispersing), the financial
sector governing the economy through ownership of claims, and algorithmic management —
software performing the foreman's functions of allocation, surveillance, discipline, and
termination, often over workers reclassified as "independent contractors" stripped of the
protections Era 7 won.
Contributed, shared, kept, accumulated. Concentration has returned toward pre-compression
levels. Current figures: the richest 1% own close to 44% of global wealth, while the poorest half
of humanity holds just over 0.5%; the top 10% of earners take more than the other 90%
combined; billionaire wealth reached ~$18.3 trillion in 2025, up ~81% since 2020, even as
roughly one in four people lack regular adequate food [measured, Oxfam / World Inequality
Report 2026]. The modern wealth Gini (~0.80–0.85 in the US) [measured] sits far above the
ancient Old World ceiling of ~0.59 — by this proxy, contemporary inequality exceeds anything in

-- 41 of 74 --

the deep past. A genuinely new category of accumulation appears: data — human behavior
itself captured as raw material, refined into prediction, and concentrated in a handful of firms.
Counter-currents. Active but, so far, not yet matching Era 7's victories. Gig workers develop
algorithmic "workaround strategies," external alliances, and new forms of organizing against
management-by-code — the union impulse reaching for new tools. Debate over a debt jubilee
has returned to serious discussion, precisely because the ancient pressure the jubilee was built
to relieve has returned without the relief. Movements for tax justice, antitrust, data rights, and a
renewed labor militancy represent the same perennial leveling impulse, now facing a
depersonalized, globalized, and partly automated adversary.
Claim to rule. Layered and increasingly contested. Formal political authority still rests on
popular sovereignty (elections, representation). But economic power rests on property,
contract, and shareholder value, and increasingly on the claim of efficiency, expertise, and
meritocracy — the assertion that those at the top are there because they are most capable or
most productive (the modern, market-flavored heir to Aristotle's "natural" hierarchy and India's
karmic rank). Oxfam's finding that billionaires are 4,000 times more likely to hold political office
than ordinary people [measured] suggests the two claims — democratic and propertied — are
fusing in ways the era has not resolved.

Reading the whole arc
Set the eight eras side by side and three patterns emerge from the numbers.
The long climb, and its one great interruption. The wealth Gini rises from the forager floor of
~0.17 through the agricultural 0.27–0.35 to the ancient ceiling of ~0.59, holds roughly there
across the classical, feudal, and Atlantic eras, then does something unprecedented in Era 7: it
falls. The mid-twentieth-century compression — driven by the strongest counter-current in
history — is the single sustained reversal of the upward trend across 12,000 years. Then Era 8
resumes the climb and overshoots everything before it, reaching ~0.80–0.85, a level of
measured wealth concentration that exceeds the steepest god-king or slave society we can
quantify. By this proxy, the present is not a return to the ancient norm but an excursion beyond
it.
Group size and archy rose together — until they didn't. For most of the arc, bigger groups
meant steeper hierarchy: the band of 25 was nearly flat, the city of 50,000 had god-kings, the
empire of 60 million had mass slavery. Scale and stratification climbed in tandem because the
tools that made large groups governable (the ledger, sacralization, the standing office) were also
tools of domination. Era 7 broke even this coupling: the largest, most legible states ever built
also achieved the most equality, because the same scale that empowered the state also
concentrated workers who could organize. Scale is not destiny; what matters is whether the
scale-tools serve extraction or get captured by the counter-current.
The claim to rule keeps migrating, but never disappears. It runs from
competence-in-the-moment (E1) to beneficence weaponized (E2) to divinity (E3) to law and
cosmic mandate (E4) to birth-plus-God (E5) to charter, conquest, and race (E6) to popular
sovereignty and property (E7) to democracy fused with property, expertise, and merit (E8). Each
new claim tends to absorb rather than replace its predecessors — the present still contains
traces of all of them. And every claim answers the same chronic problem the foragers' reverse
dominance hierarchy first posed: how can the few justify their power to the many who
outnumber them? That the question must keep being answered, in era after era, is itself the
evidence that the many never fully stopped asking it.

Part III — The Cast and the Machine
The roles and the administrative state. Parts I and II described how power works and how much
it concentrated; this part supplies fixed functional categories — six recurring roles — for
comparing eras directly, and tracks the machine those roles operate.
Why fixed roles, and which six
Parts I and II tracked mechanisms (how power is generated) and quantities (how much
inequality resulted). This part asks a different question: who, functionally, was doing what —
and can we name those functions in a way that lets us set a Sumerian temple scribe
beside a modern compliance officer and see that they are doing the same job?
The trick is to categorize by function, not by local status-label. "Noble," "citizen," "freeman,"
"shareholder" are era-specific words that don't travel. But the functions a stratified society needs
filled are remarkably stable. Six of them recur in every era from the first states onward, and
tracking who fills each one — and how the slots merge, split, and change hands — is the
clearest way to compare societies that otherwise share no vocabulary.
The six roles:
Owners — those who hold the era's primary store of value and draw income from owning it
rather than working it. What they own changes (herds, land, slaves, factories, financial claims,
data), but the relation is constant: wealth that generates more wealth, transmissible to heirs.
This is the apex role; in most eras it overlaps heavily with the next two.
Organizers — those who coordinate production and administration: who plans, allocates,
schedules, keeps the books, runs the logistics. The vizier, the steward, the bureaucrat, the

-- 46 of 74 --

manager, the platform's algorithm. They do not necessarily own; they make the system run. This
is the role that grows fastest over time and becomes, in the modern era, almost the whole story.
Enforcers — those who supply coercion: soldiers, knights, police, overseers, security. They
convert the threat of violence into compliance. Their loyalty is the hinge of every regime, which
is why so much of the surplus goes to keeping them fed and loyal, and why they so often
become Owners themselves (the warrior aristocracy).
Legitimators — those whose function is to make the arrangement feel natural, just, or
inevitable: priests, scribes, court intellectuals, jurists, and in the modern era the press, the
academy, the credentialing and ratings bodies, the PR and advertising apparatus. They produce
the stories — divine right, natural slavery, karma, meritocracy — that persuade the many to
accept rule by the few. This is the cheapest and most powerful technology of control, because a
belief internalized needs no soldier behind it.
Producers — those who do the actual work that generates the surplus: farmers, artisans,
laborers, wage workers. They are the broad base. They contribute the most and keep the least
relative to their contribution. In a flat society they are everyone; in a steep one they are the
majority who support all the roles above.
Dependents — those with no recognized economic agency of their own, whose access to
subsistence runs through someone else: the enslaved, the bonded, the destitute, the wholly
excluded. They are distinct from Producers in that Producers have at least nominal standing (a
citizen-farmer, a wage worker who can quit); Dependents have little or none. The line between
Producer and Dependent is one of the most revealing things to watch, because eras differ
sharply in how porous it is and how many people sit below it.
Two notes on using the scheme. First, one person often fills several roles at once — a feudal
lord is Owner, Enforcer, and Organizer of his manor simultaneously; a forager is, briefly and
partially, all six and none. The interesting comparative fact is precisely how bundled or
unbundled the roles are in a given era: archy tends to bundle roles at the top (the god-king is
Owner + Enforcer + Legitimator fused) and specialize them as societies grow more complex.
Second, the roles are functional slots, not moral judgments — "Legitimator" describes a job,
and the same job can be filled by a court flatterer or an honest judge.
The cast, era by era
Era 1 — Forager egalitarianism
The roles barely exist as distinct slots, which is the whole point. Everyone is a Producer;
everyone shares in what is produced. Ownership beyond portable personal goods does not

exist. Organizing is ad hoc and consensual — whoever knows the territory leads the hunt, then
stops leading. Enforcement is collective and turned upward: the band as a whole coerces any
would-be dominator (the reverse dominance hierarchy), so the enforcement function exists but
points the opposite way from every later era. Legitimation is the egalitarian ethic itself — the
stories told are leveling stories, mocking the boaster, not justifying a ruler. Dependents in the
later sense are nearly absent; the old, the young, and the sick are supported by the sharing
economy as members, not as a separate underclass. The defining feature: the roles are
unbundled and rotating, held briefly and by consent, never accumulating in one person.
Era 2 — The Neolithic threshold
The slots begin to separate. Most people remain Producers (now farmers and herders). The
novel figure is the emergent Owner-Organizer: the aggrandizer or "big man" who controls a
surplus, throws the debt-creating feast, and begins to coordinate others' labor — Owner and
Organizer fused in one ambitious household, but not yet hereditary or backed by force.
Enforcement is still mostly communal; there is no standing warrior class yet. Legitimation is
thin and contested — the communal tombs assert the collective against the aggrandizer, so the
Legitimator function is still partly working for equality. Dependents appear at the margins (the
first debt-bondsmen, the first captives) but are not yet a structural class. This is the era of
role-separation beginning but not locking: at Çatalhöyük the slots stayed fused-and-flat for a
millennium; elsewhere they pulled apart.
Era 3 — The first states and the god-kings
All six roles now exist as distinct, full-time, mostly hereditary slots — this is what "the state"
means. At the apex, the god-king fuses Owner + chief Enforcer + chief Legitimator: he
owns (or the god he embodies owns) the land, commands the army, and is the cosmic
justification. Below him the roles specialize sharply. Organizers become a true profession for
the first time: the scribe-bureaucrats who invented writing to keep the ledgers, the viziers and
overseers who ran the temple estates. Enforcers are a standing soldiery. Legitimators are a
powerful, literate priesthood — and crucially, in this era Organizer and Legitimator overlap
heavily, because the temple is simultaneously the account-house and the house of the god, so
the man keeping the grain ledger is also a priest. Producers are the peasant mass.
Dependents are now a large structural class: debt-bondsmen and slaves. The signature of the
era is maximum bundling at the very top (the god-king as everything) over newly specialized
roles below.
Era 4 — Classical empires and slavery
The apex unbundles somewhat — a Roman emperor is supreme but not literally a god in the
Egyptian sense (the imperial cult is a claim, not a metaphysics), and Han China deliberately
separates the functions. The most important development is the professionalization and partial
separation of Organizers from Owners. In Rome the senatorial Owners still dominated, but a
vast administrative apparatus of equestrian officials, tax-farmers, and freedman-bureaucrats ran
the machine. In Han China the separation went furthest: the Organizers became a distinct,

-- 48 of 74 --

text-trained scholar-official class selected partly on merit, ideally not the same people as
the great landowning Owners — the first serious attempt to make the Organizer role
independent of the Owner role. Enforcers (the legions) became so central that they repeatedly
made and unmade emperors — the enforcement role capturing the apex. Legitimators split
into a religious wing (priests, the imperial cult) and a secular wing (jurists, philosophers, the
rhetoricians who justified the order in law and reason — Aristotle's natural-slavery doctrine is
Legitimator work). Producers were free peasants and artisans; Dependents were the
enormous enslaved population, now the economic foundation rather than a marginal class. The
signature: the Organizer role professionalizes and, in the East, begins to detach from
ownership.
Era 5 — Feudal and tributary orders
After Rome's western collapse, the roles re-bundle violently in the figure of the lord. The
European feudal lord is simultaneously Owner (of the manor), Enforcer (the armed knight), and
Organizer (of the estate's production and justice) — three roles fused in one person at the local
level, which is exactly what "feudalism" is: the collapse of the specialized state back into
personal, bundled, land-based power. The Legitimator role, by contrast, specializes and
centralizes in the Church — a continent-spanning institution that monopolized the justifying
stories (divine right, the three orders) and literacy itself, and was also a massive Owner in its
own right. Producers are the serfs, bound to the land. Dependents shade into the serfs below
them and the household slaves where they persisted. Elsewhere the bundling differs
instructively: the Islamic world deliberately unbundled by using revocable iqṭāʿ grants and
slave-soldiers (mamluks) to prevent Enforcers from becoming hereditary Owners; China kept its
scholar-official Organizers separate from the military Enforcers on purpose. The signature: in
the European core, the state dissolves and the top three roles re-fuse in the local lord, while the
Legitimator role globalizes in the Church.
Era 6 — Mercantilism, colonialism, and the Atlantic system
A genuinely new kind of Owner-Organizer appears: the joint-stock company, which fuses
Owner (the shareholders) and Organizer (the directors and factors) into a single immortal legal
person — and is then granted Enforcer powers (its own armies and fleets) and quasi-sovereign
authority. For the first time the Owner-Organizer-Enforcer bundle is held not by a person but by
an abstraction, which is why responsibility disperses. The state begins to grow again behind it
(the absolutist monarchies, the chartered grants), so there are now two overlapping power
structures: the dynastic state and the corporate one, often fused. Legitimators add a dark new
product: the ideology of race, manufactured to justify the largest Dependent class in history —
the racialized enslaved, now defined as a permanent, heritable, sub-human category.
Producers in the metropole; Dependents on the plantation, with the line between them
hardened into color and made uncrossable. The signature: the Owner-Organizer-Enforcer
bundle migrates from persons to the corporation, and the Dependent class is racialized and
maximized.

Era 7 — Industrial capitalism
The roles reach their most specialized and separated configuration in history — and this
separation is the era's central drama. Ownership (capital) and Organization (management)
formally split: the rise of the salaried professional manager who runs a firm he does not own is
the defining structural novelty, the famous "separation of ownership and control." Organizers
explode into a vast new salaried middle class — managers, clerks, engineers, administrators —
who are neither Owners nor Producers in the old sense, and whose loyalty the system must
now buy. Enforcers are professionalized and nationalized: the standing police and army belong
to the state, not to private Owners (mostly). Legitimators secularize and proliferate: the press,
the universities, the advertising industry, the economics profession, nationalism itself — the
justifying stories now run through mass media and credentials rather than the pulpit. Producers
are the industrial working class, now legally free (self-owning, able to quit) but separated from
the means of production. Dependents shrink as a legal category (chattel slavery abolished,
serfdom gone) even as a new precarious underclass persists. The signature: Owner and
Organizer split, the Organizer class balloons, and the Producer becomes legally free — the
most unbundled the roles ever get. And it is precisely in this maximally specialized era that the
counter-current was strongest, because a Producer class concentrated in factories and
conscious of itself as a class could organize.
Era 8 — The present
The configuration is paradoxical: roles are simultaneously more separated (a global supply
chain disperses them across continents and legal entities) and re-fusing at the top in new ways.
Ownership re-concentrates dramatically (the return of inherited wealth and the billionaire class)
and increasingly re-merges with Organization through founder-owners and controlling
shareholders who both own and run the largest firms — a partial reversal of Era 7's separation.
The most novel development is that the Organizer role is migrating from humans into
algorithms: the platform's software now performs allocation, scheduling, surveillance, and
discipline — the steward's and foreman's job, automated and depersonalized. Enforcement
remains mostly with the state, but private security, and the "soft enforcement" of credit scores,
platform deactivation, and exclusion from digital infrastructure, supplement it. Legitimators are
the media conglomerates (increasingly owned by the apex Owners — note the billionaire
purchases of major newspapers), the ratings agencies, the credentialing universities, and the
meritocratic ideology that says the top deserves its position by talent. Producers are the global
workforce, including the gig worker reclassified out of employee protections. Dependents
include the informally employed, the platform-precarious, and — a genuinely new category —
everyone as a data-subject, whose behavioral exhaust is extracted as raw material regardless
of their consent or economic role. The signature: Owner and Organizer re-fuse at the top, the
Organizer role automates, and a new universal Dependency (data-subjecthood) overlays the old
economic ones.

The machine: the administrative state, and how private power plugs into it
The roles above describe who does what. This section tracks a single institution through the
eras — the administrative state, the standing apparatus of officials, records, and rules that can
reach into ordinary life to count, tax, conscript, register, and command — and asks two linked
questions at each step. First, how strong is the machine itself: how deeply can it actually
reach into the average person's life? Second, how does individual power tap into it: when a
private person wants to get rich or stay on top, do they do it by capturing the state, by being the
state, or by operating outside it?
The two questions matter together because the relationship between private wealth and public
administration is the master-variable of archy in the large-scale eras. A strong state can be a
leveler (it can tax the rich and protect the weak) or the ultimate tool of extraction (it can tax the
weak for the rich) — what determines which is who has captured the machine.
The state's reach, era by era
Era 1 — Forager: no machine at all. There is no administrative apparatus, no standing office,
no records. "Reach into your life" is meaningless because there is nothing to do the reaching.
Individual power cannot tap into state power because there is no state; it can only tap into
persuasion and prestige, both of which the leveling mechanisms actively cap. State strength:
zero. Private access route: none — power is personal and capped.
Era 2 — Neolithic: proto-administration. The first storage facilities, the first reckoning of who
owes a feast-debt, the first ceremonial coordination of labor (Göbekli Tepe) are the embryo of
administration, but there is still no standing apparatus that can compel a stranger. The
aggrandizer's power is personal and local — it taps into obligation and surplus, not into any
machine. State strength: minimal, embryonic. Private access route: control of surplus and the
debt of the feast.
Era 3 — First states: the machine is born, and it is owned at the top. This is the decisive
invention. Writing-as-accountancy, the temple-granary, taxation, the corvée, and the standing
bureaucracy together create, for the first time, an apparatus that can reach a peasant the king
will never meet and extract grain and labor from him. Its reach is real but shallow and patchy by
modern standards — it can tax the harvest and conscript for the corvée, but it cannot
continuously monitor daily life. Critically, the machine and the apex are fused: the king (or the
temple he fronts) owns the administrative state, so private power and state power are the same
thing at the top. There is no "capture" because there is no separation to capture across; to be
powerful is to be the state. Below the apex, the scribe-Organizers tap into state power by
serving it — the first careers built on administrative office rather than birth or force. State
strength: real but shallow. Private access route: be the apex (fused), or serve as an official.

Era 4 — Classical: the machine professionalizes and partly detaches. The Roman and
especially the Han state developed genuine bureaucratic depth — censuses (the Han counted
~57 million people, an extraordinary administrative feat), standardized law, coinage, tax rolls, a
salaried officialdom. The reach is deeper and more impersonal than Era 3: it runs on rules and
records that outlive any official. Here the relationship between private power and the state
becomes a live drama, because the two are no longer simply fused. In Rome, the great
Owner-families captured the state from within — the Senate was the apex Owners, governing in
their own interest, and tax-farming let private contractors (the publicani) turn public revenue
collection into private fortune, the purest early example of plugging private profit directly into the
state's extractive plumbing. In Han China, the deliberate counter-move: an examination-selected
official class meant partly to staff the machine with men not drawn from the great
Owner-families, an early attempt to keep the state from being simply captured by wealth. The
Enforcers (the legions) discovered they could capture the apex directly by force. State strength:
deep and impersonal. Private access routes: own the state from within (Senate), farm its
revenue (publicani), or seize it by force (legions) — with China pioneering the meritocratic
counter.
Era 5 — Feudal: the machine shatters and privatizes. The western European story is,
uniquely, one of administrative collapse. The Roman machine broke, and its functions —
taxation, justice, defense — devolved to local lords who ran them as private prerogatives. This
is the key point for your question: under feudalism, public administration and private power are
not merely connected, they are the same thing at the local level. The lord's court is his private
justice; his dues are his private tax; his men-at-arms are his private army. There is no public
machine to capture because the machine is private property, fragmented across thousands of
lordships. The slow re-building of a public administrative state — royal courts, royal taxation, the
first treasuries — across the medieval centuries is precisely the story of pulling these functions
back out of private hands into a central one. The Church, meanwhile, ran the one genuinely
trans-local administrative apparatus (canon law, the only continuous bureaucracy and literate
class), making it a quasi-state. Elsewhere the contrast is sharp: the Islamic and Chinese states
kept far stronger central administrations, deliberately using revocable grants and
examination-officials to stop Enforcers and Owners from privatizing the machine the way
European lords did. State strength: collapsed-and-privatized in the European west,
strong-and-central in the Islamic and Chinese worlds. Private access route: in the west, simply
own a fragment of the machine outright.
Era 6 — Atlantic: the dual machine, public and corporate. Two administrative apparatuses
now run in parallel and interpenetrate. The absolutist European states rebuilt strong central
administrations (standing armies, professional treasuries, the beginnings of statistics — the
word "statistics" comes from "state"). Alongside them, the chartered companies built private
administrative machines of their own — the East India Company governed millions with its own
bureaucracy, army, and courts under a grant from the state. The defining relationship of the era
is the charter: the state delegates its sovereign powers (to tax, rule, wage war, monopolize
trade) to a private profit-seeking corporation. This is a new and consequential way for private

-- 52 of 74 --

power to tap into state power — not by capturing the existing machine but by being granted a
licensed private copy of it. Individual fortunes were made by securing these charters,
monopolies, and offices; the line between public authority and private profit was deliberately
blurred. State strength: rebuilding and centralizing, but deliberately sharing its sovereignty with
corporations. Private access route: secure a charter — a licensed private piece of state power.
Era 7 — Industrial: the machine reaches everyone, and is contested. The modern
administrative state achieves, for the first time, deep and continuous reach into every individual
life. The census, the passport, compulsory schooling, conscription, the income tax, the police
force, the registry of births and deaths, the welfare bureaucracy — the state now sees and
touches the ordinary person continuously, not just at harvest. James Scott's "legibility" is
realized: the state reorganizes society into standardized, countable units it can administer. This
is the strongest the machine has ever been at reaching individuals. And here is the era's pivotal
fact for your question: because the machine became so strong, the fight over who controls it
became the central political struggle of the age. The counter-current — unions, the franchise,
mass parties — was largely a fight to capture the administrative state for the Producers: to use
its now-enormous power to tax the Owners, regulate the factories, and build the welfare state.
For a few mid-century decades they substantially succeeded, which is why this is the one era
where the strong state worked as a leveler. Private power tapped into the state through
lobbying, campaign finance, and the "revolving door" between corporate and official posts — but
it was, for a while, checked by the countervailing capture from below. State strength: maximal
reach into individual life. Private access route: lobbying, finance, the revolving door — but
contested by mass democratic capture from below.
Era 8 — Present: the machine is strong, privatized in function, and re-captured from
above. The administrative state remains immensely powerful in reach — arguably more so,
since digital records, biometric IDs, and data integration let it see individuals with a granularity
Scott never imagined. But three things have shifted the private-access story. First, functional
privatization: many administrative functions (prisons, welfare-eligibility systems, military
logistics, even surveillance) are contracted out to private firms, so private profit is again plumbed
directly into state operations, echoing the publicani and the chartered companies. Second,
regulatory capture and the financing of politics have intensified to the point that, by Oxfam's
measure, billionaires are about 4,000 times more likely to hold political office than ordinary
people, and a large share of top firms are run or principally owned by billionaires — the apex
Owners increasingly staffing and funding the machine directly, a partial return to the Roman
Senate's fusion of Owner and state. Third, a second administrative machine has grown up
outside the state entirely: the platform corporations now run private governance systems —
terms of service, algorithmic moderation, deactivation, credit and reputation scoring — that
regulate hundreds of millions of lives with state-like power but no democratic accountability, the
chartered company's private sovereignty reborn in code. The mid-century capture-from-below
has weakened as the Producer class fragmented and unions declined. State strength: maximal
and digitally enhanced, but functionally privatized and increasingly captured from above; a
parallel private machine governs alongside it.

The pattern across the whole arc
Read end to end, the administrative state traces an arc that mirrors the inequality curve but
illuminates its mechanism. The machine is born fused to the apex (E3), professionalizes and
becomes capturable (E4), shatters into private hands in the European feudal exception (E5),
rebuilds while licensing private copies of itself (E6), reaches maximal strength and becomes the
central prize fought over by mass democracy (E7), and now stands strong but functionally
privatized and re-captured from above, with a second private machine running alongside it (E8).
The throughline for individual power is this: in every era, the surest route to durable private
power is to fuse with, capture, or be granted a piece of the administrative machine —
because the machine is what converts a personal advantage into an institutional, enforceable,
heritable one. The forager could not do this because there was no machine. The god-king was
the machine. The Roman senator and the modern billionaire-office-holder capture it. The
chartered company and the platform firm get licensed or build a private copy of it. The one era
that broke the pattern — the mid-twentieth century — did so not by weakening the machine but
by democratizing who controlled it, briefly turning the strongest administrative state in history
into an instrument of leveling rather than extraction. That this was possible at all is the strongest
evidence in the whole work that the machine is a tool, not a master — and that the decisive
question is never how strong the state is, but whose hands are on it.

Tying the first three parts together
The first three parts approach the same history from three angles. Part I traced the mechanisms
— the door-opening devices (surplus, debt, the ledger, sacralization, the loss of exit) by which
power over others is generated. Part II quantified the result — group sizes, what people kept
versus surrendered, and the inequality that followed, era by era. Part III names the cast and the
machine — the six functional roles that recur across all eras, and the administrative apparatus
they operate.
Laid side by side, they converge on one claim. Archy is built when the door-opening
mechanisms switch on, and the functional roles bundle at the apex, and private power captures
or fuses with the administrative machine — and it is unbuilt, or at least tamed, when the
counter-current pries the roles apart, democratizes who holds the machine, and forces the apex
to share. The forager had no machine, unbundled roles, and dormant mechanisms, so archy
was near zero. The god-king had a new machine he personally owned, a fully bundled apex,
and most mechanisms switched on, so archy was near total. The mid-twentieth century had the
strongest machine ever built but, for a few decades, the most democratized control of it and the
most separated roles — so the same apparatus that could have been the ultimate engine of
extraction became, briefly, the great leveler.
The recurring lesson, across the first three parts, is therefore not that hierarchy is inevitable or
that it is escapable, but that it is conditional — a function of how the mechanisms, the roles, and
the machine happen to be arranged, and of how hard the many are pushing back. The
arrangement has been remade many times. It is being remade now.

Chapter 1 — The sorted: who was pushed below, and through
which structures
The first three parts spoke of "Producers" and "Dependents" as if the people in those slots were
interchangeable. They never were. Every hierarchy also sorted people by who they were — sex,
race, age, body, sexuality, belief — and assigned the worst positions accordingly. And crucially,
this sorting did not happen only through the state. It ran through a whole stack of nested
institutions, each with its own power structure: the household, the religious institution, the
school, the state, the profession/guild, the corporation, and the diffuse social sphere of
custom, reputation, and everyday interaction. A person could be a free citizen in the eyes of the
state and a subordinate in every other structure at once.
This chapter takes each axis of exclusion and asks how it operated through that stack across
the eras. The recurring finding: when one structure's grip on a group loosened, another often
tightened, so that formal liberation in one domain (say, the legal right to vote) frequently
coexisted with continued subordination in the household, the workplace, and the social sphere.
Emancipation is not one event but a structure-by-structure campaign, and it is never finished
simultaneously.
Gender: the oldest archy, nested in every structure
Patriarchy is the oldest hierarchy and the most thoroughly distributed across the institutional
stack, which is why it has been the hardest to dislodge — there is no single fortress to storm.
The household is patriarchy's home structure and its engine. The historian Gerda Lerner
argued in The Creation of Patriarchy that male control over women's reproductive and

-- 57 of 74 --

productive labor was not a byproduct of inequality but one of its sources — that the very
practice of treating people as property may have been rehearsed first on women and captives
within the household before it was generalized into class and slavery. In the forager era (E1) the
gendered division of labor existed but, in mobile bands, conferred relatively little power
difference, because women's gathering often supplied the dietary staple and nothing could be
hoarded. With the Neolithic and the first states (E2–E3), the household became the unit of
property and inheritance, and controlling that inheritance meant controlling women's
reproduction — hence the policing of female sexuality, the emergence of the bride-price and
dowry, and the legal subordination of wife to husband that runs through almost every
subsequent legal code (the Roman paterfamilias held legal power of life and death; coverture in
English common law made the married woman's legal person disappear into her husband's until
the 19th century).
The religious institution supplied the Legitimator function for gender hierarchy across most
eras and traditions — doctrines of female subordination, exclusion from the priesthood and from
religious learning, and the framing of the gendered household as divinely ordained. (The same
institutions also, at times, offered women rare avenues of authority and refuge — the abbess,
the female mystic, the religious order that let women escape marriage — a reminder that these
structures were never monolithic.)
The school was for most of history simply closed to women, which mattered enormously,
because exclusion from literacy and credentials locked women out of every role that required
them. The opening of female education (a late-19th and 20th-century achievement in most of
the world, still contested in places today) was a precondition for everything else, which is why it
is so often the first target of both reformers and reactionaries.
The state subordinated women through the denial of the vote (until the 20th-century suffrage
movements), of property rights, of the right to hold office, to sit on juries, to control their own
bodies and finances. The profession and the corporation excluded women from guilds, then
from most paid professions, then — after admitting them — subordinated them through unequal
pay, the "marriage bar" that fired women on marriage, the glass ceiling, and the unpaid "second
shift" of domestic labor that the household structure still assigned them. And the social sphere
enforced all of this through honor codes, reputation, harassment, and the dense web of
everyday custom that punished women who stepped outside assigned roles.
The pattern across the eras is uneven liberation: women won formal state rights (the vote, legal
personhood, property) across the 19th–20th centuries while remaining subordinated in the
household, the workplace, and the social sphere — which is precisely why the women's
movement had to fight on so many fronts and why "the personal is political" became its defining
insight. You cannot legislate the household free.

Race: the hierarchy that was manufactured, then naturalized
Race is the youngest of the great axes and the one whose manufacture we can almost date. As
Part I argued, premodern slavery and ethnic prejudice existed everywhere, but the idea that
humanity divides into biologically ranked races was substantially built in Era 6 to justify Atlantic
chattel slavery — an ideology produced to resolve the contradiction between Enlightenment
universalism and the brute fact of holding humans as property.
Through the structural stack: the state codified race through slave codes, then segregation
laws, citizenship and immigration restrictions by origin, and apartheid — the legal architecture of
racial subordination. The religious institution played both sides starkly: supplying biblical
justifications for slavery and segregation, and also incubating abolition and civil rights (the Black
church as the organizational heart of the American civil rights movement). The school
segregated and unequally funded, and credentialing systems reproduced racial advantage
across generations. The corporation and profession excluded by race in hiring, lending, and
housing — redlining, the color bar, occupational segregation — converting past legal
subordination into durable economic inequality even after the laws changed. And the social
sphere enforced racial hierarchy through everyday violence, exclusion, and the daily indignities
that did not require any law at all.
The cross-era lesson mirrors gender's: the legal abolition of racial hierarchy (abolition of slavery
in E6–E7, the dismantling of legal segregation and apartheid in E7–E8) ran far ahead of its
abolition in the corporate, professional, and social structures, where it persists as inherited
wealth gaps, occupational sorting, and informal exclusion. Striking down the law is necessary
and nowhere near sufficient, because the hierarchy had been built into every other structure too.
Age: the one hierarchy everyone passes through
Age is the strangest axis because, uniquely, everyone occupies every position in turn — we are
all once young, and most of us become old. This makes it the least "us versus them" of the
hierarchies and yet a real structure of power nonetheless.
The young were, across most eras, the property or near-property of the household — child labor
was the norm, not the exception, until the industrial-era reform movements (E7) made childhood
a protected category and compulsory schooling redefined the child as a student rather than a
worker. The school is the structure most specific to age: an institution built explicitly around the
subordination of the young to adult authority, which can be both developmental and disciplinary.
Children remain, in every era including our own, among the least politically enfranchised people
— unable to vote, dependent on adults' decisions, their interests represented only indirectly.
The old occupied radically different positions depending on the era's structure. In many forager
and traditional societies (E1, and traditional pockets throughout), elders held high status
precisely because, in a non-literate world, the old were the library — the repository of survival

-- 59 of 74 --

knowledge, genealogy, and precedent. The written word (E3 onward) and then the printing
press and the database progressively displaced this function: when knowledge can be stored
outside human memory, the elder's monopoly on it erodes. Industrial and present-era
economies (E7–E8), organized around wage labor and rapid skill turnover, often pushed the old
out — mandatory retirement, the devaluing of "obsolete" skills, the warehousing of the elderly
away from the productive household. The modern welfare state's pensions (an E7
counter-current achievement) were in part a response to the way industrial capitalism had
stripped the old of their former household role and income. Age status, in short, tracks the
technology of knowledge and the structure of work: where memory and continuity are valuable,
the old rise; where novelty and physical output dominate, they fall.
Disability: the axis defined by the structure itself
Disability reveals something the other axes obscure: that exclusion is often produced by the
structure, not by the body. A given impairment is disabling only relative to how a society
organizes work, space, and worth — this is the core insight of the "social model" of disability.
The same body that is excluded by a staircase is included by a ramp; the disability is in the built
environment, not only the person.
Across the eras: in subsistence societies (E1–E2), survival of people with significant
impairments depended heavily on the group's surplus and ethos, and the archaeological record
shows both care (skeletons of individuals who survived for years with conditions requiring
others' support — evidence of deep compassion) and, under scarcity, abandonment. The
religious institution framed disability variously as divine punishment, divine test, or object of
charity — the latter producing the almshouse and the hospital, structures of care that were also
structures of control and segregation. The state moved from this charity model toward, at its
worst, active persecution: the same legibility-drive that let the modern state see and serve its
population (E7) also enabled it to classify, institutionalize, sterilize, and — in the Nazi case —
murder disabled people, the eugenics movement being one of the darkest products of the
scientific Legitimator function. The corporation and profession excluded through inaccessible
workplaces and the simple assumption that the "normal" worker was non-disabled, converting
impairment into unemployment. The major counter-current is recent: the disability rights
movement of the late 20th century (E8), which reframed the issue from individual medical deficit
to societal exclusion and won, in many countries, legal mandates for accessibility and against
discrimination — a genuinely new kind of emancipation claim, asserting that the structure must
change to fit the person, not the reverse.
Sexuality and gender nonconformity: the axis of visibility
LGBTQ people present a distinct pattern: an axis where the degree of recognition itself —
whether the category was even named, criminalized, pathologized, or accepted — swung
dramatically across eras and cultures, sometimes more than the underlying acceptance.

The historical record is genuinely varied and resists a simple progress narrative. Some societies
across the eras incorporated same-sex relationships, third-gender and gender-variant roles into
their accepted structures (recognized gender-variant roles existed in numerous Indigenous
societies; same-sex relationships were woven into the social fabric of classical Greece within
specific norms; various cultures recognized third-gender categories) — while others criminalized
and persecuted them severely. The dominant structure shaping this axis shifted over time. The
religious institution was the principal definer and persecutor across many eras, framing
same-sex behavior and gender nonconformity as sin. The state then took up the function
through criminal law — sodomy laws, persecution, and in the worst cases mass murder. The
profession of medicine added, in the industrial and early modern eras (E7), a pathologizing
layer, reclassifying what had been "sin" as "mental illness," which was a mixed development —
it sometimes reduced criminal penalty while imposing a new structure of medical control (forced
"treatment," institutionalization).
The counter-current here is among the most recent and rapid in this entire analysis: the LGBTQ
rights movements of the late 20th and early 21st centuries (E8) achieved, in many societies,
decriminalization, depathologization (the removal of homosexuality from psychiatric disorder
classifications), anti-discrimination protections, and recognition of same-sex marriage — a
transformation of legal and social status faster than almost any other group's, though deeply
uneven across the world and subject to backlash. This axis is the clearest live demonstration of
the whole series' thesis that hierarchies are constructed and contingent: a category's position
can shift dramatically within a single generation, which means no hierarchy's current
arrangement is fixed.
The intersection, and the structural lesson
These axes are not independent; they compound. A person sits at the intersection of all of them
at once, and the structures stack: an enslaved woman in Era 6 was subordinated by race, sex,
and legal status simultaneously and through every institution at once — household, plantation (a
fusion of corporation and prison), church, and state. The legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw
named this intersectionality: the recognition that someone at the crossing of multiple
subordinated categories experiences not the sum but a distinct compounding of disadvantage
that single-axis remedies miss.
The structural lesson of the whole chapter is this: emancipation is not one battle but a
campaign across the entire institutional stack, and it is never won everywhere at once. A
group can be liberated by the state while still subordinated in the household, the corporation,
and the social sphere — and the unfinished structures are where the hierarchy retreats to and
regroups. This is sobering, but it is also the source of hope in the next chapter: because the
structures are many and distinct, they can be contested one at a time, and progress in one is
real even when the others lag.

Chapter 2 — Resistance without revolt: the quiet counter-current
The earlier parts tracked the dramatic counter-currents — the jubilee, Spartacus, the peasant
risings, abolition, Haiti, the unions. But open revolt is rare, dangerous, and usually crushed. For
most people in most eras, resisting the powerful by rising up was simply suicidal. So they
resisted in other ways — quieter, slower, survivable, and cumulatively powerful. This chapter
recovers that hidden counter-current, because it is where most of the actual pushing-back
happened, and because it speaks directly to the question of how abusive structures get
dismantled when you cannot storm them.
The anthropologist James C. Scott called these the "weapons of the weak": the everyday
forms of resistance available to people with no army, no vote, and no voice. Foot-dragging,
working slowly, feigned ignorance, false compliance, pilfering, sabotage, desertion, gossip,
satire, the strategic misunderstanding of orders. Scott's insight was that these are not failures to
resist — they are resistance, the form it takes when open defiance means death, and in
aggregate they impose real limits on what the powerful can extract. A field full of peasants who
all work just a little slower than they could, who let a little grain "spill," who develop a convenient
deafness when given an unreasonable order, are collectively bargaining without ever holding a
meeting.
The forms, across the eras
Flight and exit. The oldest weapon, inherited from the forager's right to walk away (E1). When
the structures permit it, leaving is the purest resistance — it withdraws the one thing the
powerful cannot do without, your labor and your body. Enslaved people in the Atlantic system
(E6) ran, and the maroon communities they founded in the mountains and swamps of the
Americas — Palmares in Brazil sheltered thousands for most of a century — were entire
societies built by people who resisted by leaving and staying gone. Serfs fled to the towns,
where (by the medieval principle that "town air makes free") a year and a day of residence could
dissolve their bondage. The peasant who slips across the border, the worker who quits, the
dissident who emigrates — all are using the forager's first weapon. Exit's power is exactly why
every archy works to abolish it (Part I's "loss of exit" thread): binding labor to land, the pass
laws, the company town, the debt that cannot be outrun. Where exit survives, the powerful are
checked; where it is closed, the quiet weapons turn inward.
Sabotage and the slowdown. Damaging the tools, the work, or the pace. The word "sabotage"
itself may descend from the wooden shoe (sabot) and the slow, clumsy work of the resentful
laborer. The slave who "accidentally" broke the plow, the factory worker who threw a shoe into
the machine, the modern employee performing the bare minimum — all impose a tax on
extraction. The slowdown and the "work-to-rule" (following every rule so literally that production
grinds to a halt) are revolt's survivable cousins: they cannot be punished as mutiny because
nothing was openly refused.

Hidden transcripts: satire, gossip, and the safe space. Scott also documented the "hidden
transcript" — the critique of power that the subordinate share among themselves, out of the
master's hearing, even while performing deference to his face. The slave spirituals with double
meanings, the peasant folktales in which the clever weak outwit the strong, the political joke
whispered under tyranny, the satire that says what cannot be said. These kept alive the
knowledge that the hierarchy was not just or natural — they preserved the leveling
consciousness of E1 underground, through eras when expressing it openly was fatal, so that it
was ready when conditions finally allowed open action. The hidden transcript is the seedbank of
the counter-current.
Building the alternative alongside. Often the most durable resistance is not attacking the
structure but building a parallel one. The mutual-aid society, the cooperative, the credit union,
the underground school, the dissident church, the free clinic, the commons held in common
against enclosure. These create, inside the shell of the hierarchical society, pockets that run on
different principles — and they double as the organizational base from which open movements
later grow (the Black church and the civil rights movement; the union hall; the safe houses of
every underground railroad). This is resistance as construction, and it is perhaps the most
hopeful form, because it does not wait for the structure to fall before beginning to live differently.
Using the master's tools: law, literacy, and the appeal upward. Subordinate groups have
repeatedly turned the powerful's own instruments against them — petitioning, litigating, invoking
the regime's stated ideals to shame it into honoring them. Enslaved and colonized people sued
for freedom in the colonizer's courts; workers used the law to enforce the rights the law
nominally granted; movements held nations to the promises in their own founding documents
("we hold these truths to be self-evident"). This is the jubilee logic (E3) inverted — instead of
relief granted from above, it is relief demanded by invoking the ruler's own legitimating story
against him. It works precisely because the Legitimator function (Part III) requires the powerful
to claim justice; once that claim is made, it can be turned into a lever.
Why the quiet counter-current matters
Three reasons this chapter is, despite its undramatic subject, the most hopeful in this work.
First, it is always available. Revolt requires conditions — concentration, opportunity, a crack in
the regime — that are rare. The quiet weapons require none of them. They are the resistance of
the powerless as such, available in the darkest eras and the tightest structures, which means
the counter-current never fully goes dormant.
Second, it is cumulatively decisive. Historians increasingly credit the day-to-day resistance of
the enslaved — the flights, the slowdowns, the sabotage, the maintenance of family and culture
against all pressure — as central to making slavery unprofitable and ungovernable, not merely
the dramatic revolts. The Black Death gave peasants leverage, but it was their willingness to

-- 63 of 74 --

use it — to leave, to demand, to refuse the old dues — that actually dissolved western serfdom
(E5). Quiet resistance sets the terms that open movements later formalize.
Third, it preserves the alternative. The hidden transcript and the parallel institutions keep
alive, through the worst eras, the knowledge and the practice of living differently. When Part I
asked whether archy was inevitable, the deepest answer is in this chapter: the leveling
consciousness of the foragers was never extinguished, because the powerless carried it
underground in their jokes, their songs, their mutual aid, and their refusals — ready to surface
whenever a crack appeared. The hope of dismantling abusive structures does not rest only on
the rare revolution. It rests on the fact that human beings, in every era and every structure, have
never fully consented to their own domination, and have always found ways — quiet, daily,
survivable — to withhold that consent and to build, in the cracks, the beginnings of something
else.
Chapter 3 — The counter-cases: societies that refused kings
The inevitability thesis lives or dies on the exceptions. If every complex society became a
hierarchy, then archy is destiny and the rest is decoration. But complex societies that stayed flat,
or that built genuine scale without permanent rulers, are scattered across the whole record —
and the first three parts kept them at the margins. This chapter brings them to the center,
because they are the strongest evidence in the entire work that hierarchy is a choice repeatedly
made and repeatedly refused, not a law of social physics.
The cases below are deliberately drawn from across continents and eras, to break the
assumption that the European-to-global trajectory was the only one available.
Stateless complexity in the deep past
We met these in Part I; here they anchor the argument. Çatalhöyük (E2) housed thousands of
fully agricultural people for roughly a millennium in near-identical houses with no palace and no
elite quarter — surplus and density without rank. The Indus Valley civilization (E3) ran some of
the largest, most sophisticated planned cities of the ancient world for centuries with no
convincing evidence of kings, royal tombs, or god-king iconography. The Ukrainian
"mega-sites" (Trypillia culture, E2) gathered thousands of people into large planned settlements
arranged in egalitarian concentric circles with no central palace or citadel — apparently
governed without a dominating center. And the Carpathian Basin communities, recent research
found, resisted the emergence of inequality for some five thousand years. None of these was a
small band that simply hadn't grown up yet; they were large, complex, durable societies that had
every supposed precondition for kingship and did not produce it.

-- 64 of 74 --

Indigenous federations and councils
The Americas before and during European contact contained a range of political forms,
including sophisticated confederations that distributed power deliberately. The Haudenosaunee
(Iroquois) Confederacy united several nations under the Great Law of Peace, governed by a
council of chiefs chosen by — and removable by — clan mothers, operating by consensus
across the member nations. Power was checked, accountable, gendered in a way that gave
women real political authority (the right to select and depose leaders), and explicitly designed to
prevent domination. Graeber and Wengrow argue that Indigenous critiques of European
hierarchy — articulated by figures like the Wendat statesman Kandiaronk in recorded debates
with Europeans — were sophisticated political philosophy that may have fed back into the
European Enlightenment's own ideas of liberty and equality. The point is not to romanticize
(these societies had their own conflicts, captures, and slaveries) but to register that
accountable, consensus-based, deliberately power-limiting governance at scale was a
developed and theorized Indigenous achievement, not a European invention.
African political diversity beyond the state
Precolonial Africa contained the full range of political forms — centralized kingdoms and
empires (Mali, Great Zimbabwe, the Asante) but also a vast number of stateless or
"acephalous" (headless) societies that organized substantial populations without centralized
rulers, through age-set systems, councils of elders, kinship networks, secret societies that
checked power, and what anthropologists call heterarchy — systems in which different
functions are governed by different overlapping bodies, so that no single hierarchy dominates all
domains. The Igbo, the Tiv, the Nuer and many others ran complex social orders with
mechanisms specifically designed to prevent the concentration of permanent authority. The
colonial powers, encountering these, frequently could not even recognize them as governance
— and so imposed chiefs ("warrant chiefs") where none had existed, manufacturing a hierarchy
to have someone to rule through. The lesson is double: stateless complex order was
widespread, and one of colonialism's functions was to destroy it and install archy where flatter
forms had prevailed.
The radically democratic margins
Even within the heart of hierarchical eras, pockets of deliberate equality appeared. Pirate ships
of the early 18th century (E6) — crewed largely by men who had fled the brutal hierarchy of the
merchant and naval marine — frequently operated under written articles that the crew signed:
the captain was elected and could be deposed, plunder was shared on a fixed and relatively flat
scale, the quartermaster checked the captain's power, and disabled crew received
compensation from a common fund. A floating society of the violently excluded built, by
deliberate agreement, one of the flatter and more democratic orders of its age — a vivid case of
people who had experienced the hierarchy at its worst designing an alternative. Comparable
experiments recur: the medieval free cities and communes, the radical-democratic moments of

-- 65 of 74 --

revolutions (the sections of revolutionary Paris, the councils and soviets before they were
captured, the anarchist collectives of 1930s Spain), the intentional communities. Most were
crushed or curdled, but their recurrence across every era is itself the evidence — the impulse to
build flat, accountable order is perennial, not exotic.
What the counter-cases establish
Three conclusions, escalating in force.
First, complex society does not require kings. The flat cities of the deep past and the
headless societies of Africa prove that scale, agriculture, trade, and durability are all compatible
with the absence of permanent centralized rule. The supposed iron chain from surplus to the
state has too many missing links.
Second, accountable power is an achievement, not a default — and it has been achieved
many times, in many places, independently. The Haudenosaunee council, the African
age-sets, the pirate articles, the revolutionary commune — these are not one tradition diffusing
outward but repeated independent inventions of mechanisms to check domination. That
recurrence is exactly what you would expect if the leveling impulse of Part I is a permanent
feature of human beings, surfacing wherever it gets the chance.
Third, archy often had to be actively imposed and the alternatives actively destroyed. The
warrant chiefs of colonial Africa, the crushing of the communes, the suppression of the maroons
— repeatedly, hierarchy did not simply emerge from complexity; it was installed, often violently,
over flatter forms that were working. This inverts the inevitability story entirely: in many times
and places, the historical labor went not into building equality against a natural tendency to
hierarchy, but into building hierarchy against a stubborn human capacity for equality.
Chapter 4 — The material drivers: technology and ecology
The first three parts told the story largely in social terms — mechanisms, roles, institutions. But
underneath the social story runs a material one: specific technologies and ecological conditions
that opened or closed the doors to power, often decisively. This chapter traces those material
drivers, because they explain why the mechanisms switched on when and where they did, and
because they puncture any purely idealist account in which hierarchy is just a matter of ideas
and choices. Ideas and choices operate within material constraints, and sometimes the material
constraint is the whole story.
A caution before the catalogue: the relationship between technology and hierarchy is not
deterministic. The same tool empowers domination in one social setting and resistance in
another — the printing press served both the censor and the heretic. Technology shifts the

-- 66 of 74 --

odds; it does not dictate the outcome. With that caveat, the material drivers fall into a few
families.
The storable-surplus complex: domestication and the plow
The foundational material driver, already central to Part I: the domestication of storable cereal
crops and herd animals (E2) created, for the first time, wealth that could be hoarded, counted,
taxed, and inherited. But the single most striking quantified material driver in this entire work is
the draft animal and the plow. The Kohler study found that the biggest divergence between
Old World and New World inequality came down to this: Eurasia had large domesticable
animals — oxen, horses, water buffalo — and the Americas did not. Draft animals let a wealthy
household work far more land than its own muscles could, pulling the plow that broke heavy
soils, which let the rich expand their holdings and created a class of landless laborers beneath
them. Inequality, the study argues, rose on both ends — the animal-owning rich got richer and
the landless poor got poorer — and this is much of why Old World Ginis climbed toward ~0.59
while New World inequality plateaued lower. A single ecological accident — which continents
happened to have rideable, yokeable mammals — shaped the divergent inequality trajectories
of entire hemispheres.
This is the material root of the "control of subsistence" mechanism (Part I). Grain is the perfect
substance for archy: it ripens predictably (so it can be taxed on a schedule), it stores (so it can
be hoarded and controlled), and it is visible and divisible (so it can be assessed and seized).
The anthropologist James Scott argued that early states formed specifically around cereal
agriculture for exactly these reasons — that you cannot build a tax-collecting state on cassava
or potatoes that hide underground and can be dug as needed, but you can build one on wheat
standing assessable in a field. The crop's biology shaped the politics.
The metals and the mounted warrior
Bronze and then iron metallurgy (late E2 into E3–E4) did two opposed things. Iron, once cheap,
democratized tools and weapons — an iron blade in many hands. But the warrior complex it
enabled — bronze weapons, then the horse, the chariot, and eventually the mounted, armored
cavalryman — created a military aristocracy. The Kohler researchers specifically credit the
arrival of bronze metallurgy and a mounted warrior elite with driving Old World inequality up
through large houses and territorial conquest. The horse in particular is a recurring engine of
domination: it gave its possessors a decisive military and mobility advantage, underwrote the
steppe empires and the conquering cavalries, and made the Enforcer role (Part III) physically
dominant. The feudal knight (E5) is, materially, a man who can afford a warhorse and armor —
the whole three-orders hierarchy rests partly on the expense of mounted warfare.
The technologies of legibility: writing, printing, the database
Document three's "ledger" mechanism has a precise material history. Writing (E3), born as
accountancy, gave power its memory — the ability to record what is owed and owned beyond

the limits of any human mind. The printing press (15th century, late E5 into E6) is the great
double-edged case: it served the state and church (mass-produced propaganda, standardized
scripture, bureaucratic forms) and shattered their monopoly on the Legitimator function — it
powered the Reformation by putting the Bible in vernacular hands, spread the pamphlets of
every subsequent revolution, and created the public sphere in which the powerful could be
criticized at scale. The same technology centralized and decentralized authority at once. The
census and statistics (E7) made whole populations legible. And the database and the
networked sensor (E8) complete the arc, giving power not just memory but prediction — the
capacity to know individuals with a granularity that earlier states could only dream of. Each step
in this family extended the reach of the ledger; each was also, in turn, seized by the
counter-current as a tool of resistance (the dissident's printing press, the activist's network).
The energy revolutions
The deepest material driver of the modern eras is energy. Human and animal muscle, then
water and wind, then — transformatively — fossil fuels (E7). The steam engine and then the
internal combustion engine and electrification multiplied the power available to society by orders
of magnitude, and this is the material foundation of industrial capitalism: the factory, the railroad,
the mechanized everything. Energy abundance enabled the population explosion, the vast new
scale of production, and the surplus that funded both the worst exploitation and, eventually, the
welfare state. It also concentrated a new kind of power in whoever controlled the energy
sources — the coal seam, the oil field — adding a new resource to the list of things over which
archy is built. The current contest over energy (and the ecological limits fossil energy has
brought us to) is, in this frame, the latest chapter of a story in which who controls the society's
power supply shapes who holds power over its people.
Ecology as constraint and shock
Finally, ecology operates not just as resource but as constraint and shock. Climate shifts
repeatedly destabilized regimes (drought and the collapse of several Bronze Age states; the
cold spells that triggered famines and revolts). Disease, as Chapter 5 will press, was one of
history's great levelers — the Black Death (E5) killed enough people to invert the land-labor
ratio and dissolve western serfdom. Geography shaped what was possible: societies with
access to defensible mountains and swamps gave their maroons and rebels somewhere to flee
(preserving exit); societies on open plains were easier to conquer and tax. The material world
set the board on which the social game of archy was played.
The lesson: material odds, social choices
The material drivers explain the timing and the odds — why hierarchy became available when
and where it did, why some continents grew more unequal than others, why certain
technologies tilted the field toward domination or toward resistance. But they do not, by
themselves, dictate the outcome, and the counter-cases of Chapter 3 prove it: Çatalhöyük had
the grain and stayed flat; the Indus had the cities and seemingly no kings. The honest synthesis

-- 68 of 74 --

is that technology and ecology load the dice, and the social mechanisms and human
choices of the first three parts throw them. Neither the purely material nor the purely social
account is complete. Archy is what happens when a particular material configuration meets a
particular social one — and changing either can change the result, which is why the present
era's combination of new energy constraints and new information technologies makes the
current arrangement, like every previous one, genuinely up for grabs.
Part V — The Case Against This Analysis
The honest objections. The preceding four parts built and complicated an argument; this part
turns the strongest available objections against the whole of it, and concedes what an honest
reader might conclude instead.
A work that argues a position should be able to make the strongest possible case against itself.
This whole inquiry leans toward a particular reading: that archy is a contingent deviation from a
human capacity for equality, that it is built rather than natural, and that it can therefore be
unbuilt. That reading is contestable, and an honest account owes the objections their full force
— not as straw men to be knocked down, but as serious arguments that a thoughtful reader
might find more convincing than the thesis. This part steelmans five of them.
Objection 1: Hierarchy is natural, not a deviation
The series treats forager equality as the human baseline and archy as the deviation. The
opposing view, with real evidence behind it, runs the other way: we are primates, and primates
build dominance hierarchies. Our closest relatives, chimpanzees, live in steep, often violent
status hierarchies; status-seeking, coalition-building, and the drive to rise are deeply rooted in
primate (and human) psychology. On this reading, forager equality is not the natural human
state but a hard-won, fragile suppression of our natural tendency to dominate — which is
exactly why foragers needed all those active leveling mechanisms (the ridicule, the
demand-sharing, the threat of ostracism). You don't need elaborate machinery to enforce a
tendency that comes naturally; you need it to suppress one that doesn't. The very existence of
the leveling toolkit, the objection says, proves that the dominance drive was always there,
straining against the leash. And the moment conditions allowed — surplus, sedentism —
hierarchy reasserted itself everywhere, because it was the default all along.
The series' best reply is that the capacity for both is real, and that the leveling machinery proves
humans can and did choose equality for the vast majority of our history — but the objection has
force, and it should unsettle anyone too confident that equality is our "true" nature waiting to be
liberated.

-- 69 of 74 --

Objection 2: Hierarchy is efficient, and it funded everything good
This work frames archy almost entirely as extraction and domination. But there is a powerful
functionalist case that hierarchy earns its keep. Coordination at scale requires someone to
coordinate; large projects (the irrigation systems, the granaries that buffered famine, the roads,
the defense against raiders) require concentrated authority to organize them. The surplus that
elites extracted also funded the specialists — the metallurgists, the astronomers, the scribes,
the artists, the philosophers — whose work created the science, technology, literature, and art
we treasure. Division of labor, which requires that some people not farm, is the engine of all
cultural and material progress, and it is inseparable from some people directing others. On this
view, the forager equality this work admires came bundled with permanent poverty, no writing,
no medicine, no accumulated knowledge — a flat society precisely because it was a society
with almost nothing to be unequal about. The god-king was monstrous, but the granary he
controlled also kept his people alive through the lean years, and the civilization he taxed into
being is the one that eventually produced the very ideas of rights and equality this work
champions. You may not be able to have the Enlightenment without first having the pharaohs.
The series can reply that the counter-cases (Chapter 3) show much of this coordination was
achievable without permanent rulers, and that the "hierarchy funded progress" story
conveniently credits the elite for the work of the many. But the functionalist case is not absurd,
and the burden is on the egalitarian to show that complex coordination and accumulated
knowledge can be sustained at scale without some concentration of authority — a thing more
often asserted than demonstrated.
Objection 3: Inequality is the price of innovation and freedom
A narrower modern version of the efficiency argument: that some inequality is not just tolerable
but necessary and even just. If people are free to work, create, invent, and trade, they will
produce unequal results, because human talents and efforts are unequal — and the alternative,
enforced equality of outcome, requires exactly the kind of coercive central power this work
otherwise deplores. The 20th century's attempts to abolish inequality by force (the communist
revolutions) produced not equality but new and often murderous hierarchies of party and state,
and immense human suffering. The incentive to rise — to get rich by building something people
want — drives the innovation that has, on this account, lifted billions out of the absolute poverty
that was the real condition of the egalitarian past. Inequality of reward is the engine; flattening it
stalls the engine. A society that lets no one rise far above the rest may be equal, but it may also
be stagnant and poor, and its equality may require a boot on every neck to maintain.
This objection cuts deep because it turns this work's own values against it: the coercion it fears
is precisely what enforced equality has historically required. The reply is that there is a vast
space between "some inequality from genuine free contribution" and the present concentration
where a few thousand people hold more than half of humanity — that the question is not
equality-versus-inequality but how much, from what, and with what mobility. But the objection

rightly warns that "abolish inequality" is a dangerous slogan if it is not disciplined by the question
of what replaces it.
Objection 4: The egalitarian past is romanticized
This work leans on forager equality and the stateless counter-cases as proof that things can be
otherwise. The objection: this romanticizes societies that were, by most measures we care
about, grim. Forager and stateless life often meant short lifespans, high child mortality, chronic
vulnerability to famine and weather, and — contrary to the "peaceful savage" image — rates of
violent death from raiding and feud that, as a proportion of the population, often exceeded those
of the modern states this work criticizes. The "equality" was partly the equality of shared poverty
and shared danger. The leveling mechanisms this work admires included, at the extreme, killing
people who got too big — egalitarianism enforced by homicide. The Indigenous and stateless
societies held up as alternatives had their own slaveries, their own patriarchies, their own
captures and cruelties. Selecting the flat and accountable cases (the Haudenosaunee council,
the pirate articles) while passing quickly over the violent and hierarchical ones (and many
stateless societies were neither peaceful nor equal) is cherry-picking in service of a thesis.
The series' honest reply is that it has tried to flag these (the forager execution of upstarts, the
"don't romanticize" cautions in Chapter 3) — but the objection is correct that the selection of
cases tilts hopeful, and that a fully honest account must weigh the brutal stateless societies as
heavily as the admirable ones.
Objection 5: Inequality never falls without catastrophe — so hope is
misplaced
This is the gravest objection, and it comes from the historian Walter Scheidel, whose The Great
Leveler surveys the same sweep of history this work does and reaches a far bleaker conclusion.
Scheidel argues that since humans began to farm, herd, and pass on assets, inequality has
been a defining feature of civilization, and over thousands of years only violent events have
significantly lessened it. He identifies "Four Horsemen" of leveling — mass-mobilization warfare,
transformative revolution, state collapse, and catastrophic plague — that have repeatedly
destroyed the fortunes of the rich, and argues that every major reduction in economic inequality
has been linked to some massive, violent shock that upended the established order. The
peaceful reforms this work celebrates — the unions, the franchise, the welfare state — were
not, on Scheidel's account, autonomous victories of the counter-current at all: the
mid-20th-century compression was largely a product of the social solidarity and upheaval
created by the two World Wars, and once that shock faded, after 1945 tax rates on the
wealthiest started falling and inequality resumed its climb. The leveling of the Black Death (E5),
too, worked by killing enough people to change the ratio of land to labor, raising wages — and
was slowly reversed as populations rebounded.

If Scheidel is right, the entire hopeful arc of this series is an illusion. The quiet resistance of
Chapter 2, the counter-cases of Chapter 3, the democratic capture of the mid-century — none
of it represents a peaceful path to equality, because there is no peaceful path. Equality has only
ever been bought with mass death, and it has never lasted. And the modern good news — that
the Four Horsemen have "dismounted their steeds," as mass-mobilization war, transformative
revolution, state collapse, and civilization-ending plague all look unlikely — is, on Scheidel's
reading, also the bad news: the very stability that spares us catastrophe also removes the only
force that has ever flattened the pyramid. We may be stuck with rising inequality precisely
because we are, mercifully, no longer subject to the horrors that once reduced it.
The series owes this its most serious engagement, because it is built on the same evidence and
is not easily dismissed. Three replies are available, and none is a knockout. First, measurement:
Scheidel's focus on material wealth inequality may understate the genuine, durable, and largely
peaceful gains in legal and social hierarchy — the abolition of slavery, of coverture, of legal
caste, of segregation, the enfranchisement of women, the decriminalization of being gay —
which are real reductions in archy (the power of one over another) even where wealth Ginis kept
climbing. The series defined archy more broadly than wealth, and on that broader measure the
peaceful counter-current has clear, lasting wins. Second, causation is contested: other scholars
argue Scheidel underweights the autonomous role of organized politics, ideas, and institutions,
and that the violence and the reform were intertwined rather than the violence being the sole
cause. Third, the future is not the past: the material drivers of Chapter 4 are shifting — new
energy systems, new information technologies, new ecological limits — and a future that does
not resemble the agrarian past may not be bound by its iron law, for better or worse.
But intellectual honesty requires the concession: Scheidel may simply be right, and the
hopeful reading wrong. It is entirely possible that the peaceful taming of archy is rare, fragile,
and shock-dependent, and that the comfortable stability of the present is also a trap. The series'
hope is defensible, but it is not certain, and a reader persuaded by Scheidel has every right to
find this whole project's optimism unearned.
Why the objections strengthen rather than destroy the analysis
Holding these five objections together, what survives of this work's thesis? Not the strongest
version — not "equality is our true nature and archy is a removable error." What survives is the
weaker, more defensible claim the work actually built toward: that archy is conditional, not
inevitable; that humans demonstrably carry both the capacity to dominate and the
capacity to level; that which one prevails depends on mechanisms, roles, material
conditions, and choices that have been arranged many different ways; and that the
arrangement is therefore, in principle, contestable — though contesting it peacefully and
durably is far harder than the hopeful reading suggests, and may be rarer than we would
like to believe.

That is a more modest thesis than this work sometimes reaches for, and the objections are what
discipline it down to honesty. The strongest case against the analysis does not destroy it; it
strips away its overconfidence and leaves the defensible core — which is, in the end, the most
that history honestly supports: not that we are destined for equality, nor that we are doomed to
hierarchy, but that we have always been, and remain, the animal that builds both and must keep
choosing between them.
Conclusion to the combined volume
The five parts approach one history from five angles, and it is worth drawing them back
together.
Part I traced the mechanisms — the door-opening devices by which power over others is
generated, and the leveling devices that resist them — and established that sustained equality,
not hierarchy, was the human baseline. Part II made that quantitative, measuring group sizes,
what people kept versus surrendered, and the inequality that followed, era by era. Part III
supplied fixed categories — six functional roles and the administrative machine they operate —
and showed that archy concentrates when the roles bundle at the apex and the machine is
captured from above. Part IV cut across the eras: it disaggregated "the many" into the specific
groups every hierarchy sorted to the bottom and showed their subordination ran through the
whole institutional stack, so that emancipation has always been a campaign across many fronts,
never won everywhere at once; it recovered the quiet counter-current — flight, sabotage, the
hidden transcript, the parallel institution — that carried the leveling impulse through eras when
open revolt meant death; it put the stateless and non-Western societies that refused kings at the
center, finding that complex society never required rulers and that archy often had to be actively
imposed over flatter forms that worked; and it added the material drivers — the plow, the horse,
the press, the engine, the database, the ecological shocks — that loaded the dice the social
mechanisms then threw. And Part V turned the strongest objections against the whole project,
conceding that the hopeful reading may be wrong, that hierarchy may be natural and efficient
and reducible only by catastrophe, and that what honestly survives is a more modest thesis than
the work sometimes reached for.
That modest, disciplined thesis is the real conclusion, and it is worth stating once more in its
chastened form. Archy — the power of one human over another — is neither our destiny nor our
error to simply correct. It is conditional: a product of mechanisms, roles, material conditions, and
choices that have been arranged in radically different ways across the human story, from the flat
band to the god-king to the brief mid-century taming to the present re-concentration. The
excluded have always resisted, usually quietly; the alternatives have always existed, often
crushed; the material world has always shaped the odds; and the question has never been
settled. We are the animal that builds both hierarchy and its undoing, and the evidence of the

means it is open now, as it has always been.
